## Summary
### Lecture 1: Tennessee's Physical Landscape & Deep Time — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Physical Geography & Regions
* **Dimensions of Tennessee:** Roughly **440 miles** east to west and **112 miles** north to south (figures vary by source) — a long, narrow state whose varied terrain shapes its human history, especially in the first half of the course.
* **Unaka Mountains:** A sub-range of the **Appalachian Mountains** along Tennessee's eastern border; the Appalachians stretch from New England down into the Deep South.
* **Ridge and Valley / Great Valley:** The distinctive alternating ridges and valleys of East Tennessee, including the Great Valley just west of the mountains.
* **Cumberland Plateau:** A 40–50-mile-wide, highly visible plateau separating East Tennessee from Middle Tennessee.
* **Eastern & Western Highland Rim:** The upland rims surrounding Middle Tennessee, west of the Cumberland Plateau.
* **Western Valley of the Tennessee River:** The valley running through West Tennessee before the land drops toward the Mississippi.
* **Mississippi River Floodplain:** The westernmost region, known for its rich alluvial soil.
* **Three Grand Divisions:** Tennessee is legally divided into **East, Middle, and West Tennessee**; the lecturer uses these labels throughout the course.

##### Natural Resources & Rivers
* **Phosphate-rich limestone:** Characteristic bedrock of Middle Tennessee.
* **Alluvial soil:** Fertile river-deposited soil of the Mississippi floodplain.
* **Mineral resources:** **Iron** and **marble** in the Great Valley, **copper** in the south near the Unaka range, and **coal** in the Cumberland Plateau.
* **Three major rivers:** The **Mississippi**, the **Tennessee**, and the **Cumberland**.

##### Historical Method & Deep Time
* **Prehistory:** For historians, the period before written records exist. Because written documents are the core of historical analysis, earlier eras must be studied through other evidence (archaeology, geology).
* **Anthropological vs. geological timescales:** Anthropologists date Tennessee's human beginnings to about **12,000–13,000 years ago**; geologists look back hundreds of millions of years.
* **Deep time:** The lecturer's argument that human and material (natural) history are not truly separate — understanding a place requires filling in its geological past.

##### Geological Eras & Ancient Environments
* **Formation (~1 billion years ago):** The land that became Tennessee began forming while underwater; the metamorphic rocks exposed in the **Blue Ridge Mountains** date from this period.
* **Paleozoic Era:** Tennessee was covered by a shallow sea full of marine life (preserved as fossils). Mountain building created sediment and swampy deltas whose plant matter became Tennessee's **coal deposits**. By the era's end most of the state was above sea level.
* **Mesozoic Era:** Only **West Tennessee** remained under shallow seawater, home to clams, oysters, and snails; the rest of the state held swamps and forests.
* **Ice Age / later prehistoric fauna:** Prehistoric elephants, alligators, and **mastodons** roamed the state. Mastodon remains show that even though ice sheets never reached Tennessee, the climate was cooler.

##### The Gray Fossil Site
* **Gray Fossil Site:** Near the town of **Gray** in East Tennessee; discovered in **2000** by a state road construction crew and now a research center run by **East Tennessee State University (ETSU)**.
* **Forested pond ecosystem:** Preserves a pond and forest from about **4–5 million years ago** (early **Pliocene**), when East Tennessee was slightly warmer. Remains were preserved in a water-filled **sinkhole** in the limestone.
* **200+ species:** Plants and animals found there include alligators, mastodons, rhinos, and a fossil **red panda**, a species unique in the Americas.
* **New rhinoceros species:** The site's most significant find: an extinct rhino with a distinctive body structure (especially the legs), found nowhere else in the world.
* **"Little Guy" (bonus word):** The name of one of the complete rhino skeletons from the Gray Fossil Site.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Geology Shapes History:** Hundreds of millions of years of submersion, sedimentation, and mountain building produced the terrain, soils, minerals, and rivers that later shaped where and how people lived in Tennessee.
2. **The Receding Sea:** Tennessee rose from the sea east to west. West Tennessee stayed underwater into the Mesozoic, which helps explain its low floodplain landscape compared with the mountainous east.
3. **History vs. Prehistory:** Historians depend on written records, so the long human and geological past before writing counts as "prehistory" and must be reconstructed from material evidence.
4. **An Evolving Past:** Discoveries like the Gray Fossil Site show that knowledge of Tennessee's deep past, like its human history, keeps changing as new evidence is found.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This opening lecture introduces Tennessee's physical landscape and its geological "deep time," arguing that the state's material features are essential to understanding its human history.

1. **Tennessee's Physical Landscape:** Tennessee stretches about **440 miles** east to west and **112 miles** north to south. Moving west from the **Unaka Mountains** (part of the **Appalachians**), the land passes through the **Ridge and Valley** region and **Great Valley**, the 40–50-mile-wide **Cumberland Plateau**, the **Highland Rim**, the **Tennessee River Valley**, and finally the **Mississippi River floodplain**. The state holds phosphate-rich **limestone** in Middle Tennessee, fertile **alluvial soil** in the west, **iron** and **marble** in the Great Valley, **copper** near the Unakas, and **coal** in the Cumberland Plateau, along with three major rivers: the **Mississippi**, **Tennessee**, and **Cumberland**. Legally, the state is split into three **Grand Divisions** (East, Middle, and West), which the course uses as its regional labels.
2. **Defining Prehistory:** Historians study the past mainly through written documents, so everything before written records is **prehistory**. Anthropologists date Tennessee's human story to about **12,000–13,000 years ago**, while geologists think in hundreds of millions of years. The lecturer argues that human and material history are closely linked, so the course starts by filling in this **deep time** background.
3. **Tennessee's Geological Past (~1 billion years ago – Ice Age):** The land began forming about **1 billion years ago** underwater, leaving the metamorphic rocks now exposed in the **Blue Ridge Mountains**. During the **Paleozoic Era**, a shallow sea full of marine life covered the state, and swampy deltas created by mountain building eventually became Tennessee's **coal** deposits. By the end of the Paleozoic most of the state stood above sea level. In the **Mesozoic**, only **West Tennessee** remained under shallow seawater, while swamps and forests elsewhere supported prehistoric elephants, alligators, and later **mastodons**, evidence of a cooler Ice Age climate even though glaciers never reached Tennessee.
4. **The Gray Fossil Site (discovered 2000; ~4–5 million years ago):** In **2000**, a road construction crew near **Gray** in East Tennessee found fossils, and the site became a research center run by **ETSU**. It preserves a forested pond ecosystem from the early **Pliocene**, trapped in a water-filled **sinkhole** in the limestone, with more than **200 species** including alligators, mastodons, rhinos, and a **red panda** unique in the Americas. Its most important find is a **new species of extinct rhinoceros**, found nowhere else in the world and notable for its unusual legs; one complete skeleton is nicknamed **"Little Guy"** (the lecture's bonus word). The site shows that, like human history, Tennessee's deep past is still being rewritten as new evidence appears.

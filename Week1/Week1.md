# Week 1 Summary & Study Focus

**Big picture:** Week 1 moves from Tennessee's physical landscape into "deep time" — first the hundreds of millions of years of geology that built the land, then the roughly 13,000-year human history of the region before it was ever "Tennessee." The two First Tennesseans lectures trace Native societies from the earliest hunters through the arrival of Europeans and the upheaval that followed, ending right at the doorstep of the historic tribal nations (Cherokee, Chickasaw, Creek, Choctaw) that the rest of the course will keep coming back to.

### Quick recap
- **Lecture 1 – Tennessee History & Deep Time:** Orienting facts — the state's dimensions (~440 mi E–W, 112 mi N–S), its landforms from the Unaka Mountains west to the Mississippi floodplain, its natural resources (limestone, alluvial soil, iron, copper, coal, marble), its three major rivers (Cumberland, Mississippi, Tennessee), and the three Grand Divisions (East, Middle, West). Flags a key methods point: historians rely on written records, so everything before them counts as "prehistory" and has to be reconstructed from material evidence. Then covers deep time: the land formed underwater ~1 billion years ago, a Paleozoic shallow sea left marine fossils and coal, West Tennessee stayed underwater into the Mesozoic, and the Gray Fossil Site (found in 2000 and run by ETSU) preserves a ~4–5-million-year-old pond ecosystem with a red panda and a new rhino species ("Little Guy").
- **Lecture 2 – The First Tennesseans, pt. 1:** The long cultural sequence of Native life in Tennessee — Paleo-Indian → Archaic → Woodland → Mississippian (~13,000 BP to ~1600 CE) — capped by the arrival of Hernando de Soto in 1540, the first major European contact.
- **Lecture 3 – The First Tennesseans, pt. 2:** What de Soto's arrival set in motion — the "Mississippian Shatter Zone" (historian Robbie Ethridge's term), roughly 1540–1715, when disease, trade dependency, and the Indian slave trade destroyed the small pre-contact chiefdoms and forced survivors to coalesce into the large historic nations that would dominate the 18th-century South.

### What to focus on
1. **The four-stage cultural sequence** (Paleo-Indian → Archaic → Woodland → Mississippian) and roughly what changes at each stage — mobility, subsistence, settlement size, social complexity. This is the backbone Lecture 2 is built on.
2. **The Shatter Zone concept** — this is the week's central analytical idea, not just a list of bad things that happened. Be able to explain its *causes* (disease, the guns-for-slaves trade cycle, warfare, incorporation into a European-dominated economy) and its *outcome* (coalescence).
3. **Coalescence** — why and how small, decentralized Mississippian polities consolidated into the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Creek, and Choctaw by the 1700s. This is the throughline connecting pt. 1 and pt. 2.
4. **Anchor dates** — 1540 (de Soto / shatter zone begins), 1696 (smallpox outbreak), 1715 (Yamasee War, shatter zone ends, "Colonial South" emerges). These three dates are enough to place everything else in sequence.
5. **Geography → history (Lecture 1)** — the landforms from east to west, where the resources are, and the three Grand Divisions, since later lectures use these as shorthand. Also know why "prehistory" is defined by the lack of written records, and the Gray Fossil Site as proof that the deep past is still being rediscovered.
6. **The Cherokee's specific arc** — initially avoided the slave trade, then armed their own slave raiders around 1700, then emerged as the dominant Native power allied with the English. Worth knowing well since the Cherokee recur throughout the rest of the course.

# Week 3 Summary & Study Focus

**Big picture:** Week 3 closes out the frontier era and carries Tennessee from squatter settlements through the failed State of Franklin, the federally organized Southwest Territory, and finally statehood in 1796 — ending with the land cessions and early political rivalries (Sevier, Blount, Jackson) that shaped the new state's first two decades.

### Quick recap
- **Lecture 4, pt. 4 – The Frontier:** A short lecture on how outsiders labeled backcountry settlers "squatters" and "crackers," contrasting them with Thomas Jefferson's yeoman ideal (via historian Nancy Isenberg) — labels that would later curdle into a distinct, politically potent Appalachian identity.
- **Lecture 4, pt. 5 – The Frontier:** The 1779–1780 settlement of the Cumberland region by two parties — Robertson's overland group and Donelson's disaster-prone 1,000-mile river flotilla — ending in the founding of the Cumberland Compact and Davidson County.
- **Lecture 4, pt. 6 – The Frontier ("The Lost State of Franklin"):** North Carolina's 1784 cession of its western lands triggers a homegrown statehood bid under Governor John Sevier. Franklin operates as a de facto state for several years but never wins recognition, and collapses in 1787–1788 after John Tipton's pro–North Carolina faction has Sevier arrested for treason.
- **Lecture 5, pt. 1 – Southwest Territory:** North Carolina cedes the same land to the federal government instead, forming the Southwest Territory under Governor William Blount. The 1791 Treaty of Holston opens more land to settlement, and population growth (tracked through two censuses and a referendum) clears the path to a 1796 constitutional convention in Knoxville.
- **Lecture 5, pt. 2 – Early Statehood:** Tennessee is admitted as the 16th state on June 1, 1796, over Federalist objections. Sevier becomes governor, Blount is elected senator (then expelled over a 1797 conspiracy), and Andrew Jackson enters national politics — setting up his rivalry with Sevier. The chapter closes with the 1818 Chickasaw Cession opening West Tennessee to settlement.

### What to focus on
1. **The statehood sequence, not just the events:** Squatter settlements → Franklin (unrecognized, collapses) → Southwest Territory (federally recognized) → statehood (1796). Each stage fails or succeeds for a specific structural reason — Franklin lacked recognition from any higher authority; the Southwest Territory had it.
2. **Land cession as the throughline:** Sycamore Shoals (Week 2) → Treaty of Holston (1791) → 1798 Cherokee cession → 1818 Chickasaw Cession. Recognizing this as one continuous, escalating pattern — not isolated treaties — ties the whole course together and sets up Indian Removal in the 1830s.
3. **Population thresholds as a real mechanism:** The Southwest Territory's path to statehood was gated by specific numeric requirements (5,000 adult men for a legislature, 60,000 free inhabitants for statehood, per the Northwest Ordinance model) — this wasn't just political will, it was a bureaucratic process with hard numbers attached.
4. **Personal rivalries shaping early state politics:** Sevier vs. Blount, then Sevier vs. Jackson (near-duel, 1803) — early Tennessee politics ran on personal feuds and reputational combat as much as policy. Jackson's emergence here is the seed of his much larger role next week.
5. **Key names to anchor the narrative:** **John Sevier** (Franklin governor → Tennessee's first governor), **William Blount** (territorial governor → senator → disgraced conspirator), **Andrew Jackson** (first U.S. Representative, rising political star), **John Tipton** (led Franklin's opposition), and historian **Nancy Isenberg** (squatter/cracker framing).
6. **Anchor dates:** 1779–1780 (Cumberland settlement), 1784–1788 (State of Franklin), 1790 (Southwest Territory established), 1791 (Treaty of Holston), June 1, 1796 (Tennessee statehood), 1797 (Blount Conspiracy), 1803 (Jackson-Sevier near-duel), 1818 (Chickasaw Cession).

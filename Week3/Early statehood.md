### Lecture 5, Part 2: Early Statehood — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Tennessee's Admission as a State (1796)
* **Federalist Opposition to Statehood:** Federalists opposed Tennessee's admission largely because they expected the new state to support Thomas Jefferson (a Democratic-Republican) over their own candidate, John Adams, in the 1796 presidential election, and argued Congress should authorize a state's formation before a constitution was drafted, not after.
* **Albert Gallatin and James Madison:** Congressional advocates who pushed forward Tennessee's bid for statehood despite Federalist resistance.
* **June 1, 1796:** The official date Tennessee was admitted as the 16th state, following House approval (May 6) and Senate approval (May 31) that reduced Tennessee's initial House representation to one seat as a condition of admission.

##### Early State Politics & Rivalries
* **John Sevier:** Tennessee's first governor (1796–1801, then again 1803–1809, the constitutional maximum of three consecutive terms), whose folk-hero status from his frontier and Franklin-era career translated directly into political power.
* **William Blount:** Elected to the U.S. Senate in 1796; later at the center of a 1797 conspiracy (the "Blount Conspiracy") to use British money and military assistance to help Britain seize Spanish Florida and Louisiana. Expelled from the Senate and pursued on an arrest warrant, he evaded capture, returned to Knoxville to a surprisingly warm welcome, was later elected to the Tennessee state senate, and died in 1800.
* **Andrew Jackson:** Elected as Tennessee's first U.S. Representative in 1796; his rivalry with Sevier escalated over the following years — including Jackson's exposure of Sevier's allegedly shady land dealings — nearly resulting in a duel between the two men in 1803. Jackson's own reputation for dueling and violent confrontation grew alongside a loyal popular following during this period.

##### Growth, Institutions, and Land Cessions
* **1800 Federal Census:** Recorded Tennessee's total population at roughly 106,000, with a substantial and growing enslaved population concentrated mostly in Middle Tennessee.
* **Shifting State Capital:** The capital moved from Knoxville to Nashville (1812), back to Knoxville, then to Murfreesboro (1819), and finally permanently to Nashville (1826), reflecting a broader shift of political power from East to Middle/West Tennessee (the last East Tennessee governor served in the 1810s).
* **Early Institution-Building:** Blount College, founded in Knoxville in 1794, became East Tennessee College in 1807 and eventually the University of Tennessee; the Knoxville Gazette was among the earliest Tennessee newspapers.
* **Continued Cherokee and Chickasaw Land Cessions:** A series of treaties — including an October 1798 Cherokee cession and, above all, the 1818 Chickasaw Cession (the "Jackson Purchase") — progressively opened West Tennessee to settlement.
* **The Chickasaw Cession of 1818:** Negotiated by Andrew Jackson and Kentucky governor Isaac Shelby, this treaty (using bribery among its tactics) ceded Chickasaw claims to West Tennessee and parts of West Kentucky in exchange for $300,000, the largest single land cession in Tennessee by any tribe; it led directly to the creation of Shelby and Hardin Counties and a rush of settlement and speculation.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Statehood as Partisan Calculation:** Federalist opposition to Tennessee's admission was driven less by procedural principle than by the calculation that a new frontier state would vote for Jefferson — an early example of statehood decisions being shaped by national partisan politics.
2. **Personal Rivalry as the Engine of Early State Politics:** The unresolved rivalry between Sevier and Jackson — and, earlier, between Sevier and Blount — shows that Tennessee's earliest political history was driven as much by personal feuds and reputational combat (near-duels, exposed scandals) as by policy.
3. **Land Cession as the Constant Throughline:** Just as in the frontier era, Tennessee's growth after statehood continued to be driven by a steady sequence of treaties and cessions — culminating in the massive 1818 Chickasaw Cession — that opened new territory even as they steadily shrank Indigenous land, foreshadowing the forced removals of the 1830s.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture covers Tennessee's first years as a state, from admission in 1796 through the land cessions that opened West Tennessee in the early 1800s.

1. **Admission to the Union (1796):** Despite Federalist opposition — largely rooted in the expectation that Tennessee would support Thomas Jefferson over John Adams — advocates like **Albert Gallatin** and **James Madison** helped secure Tennessee's admission as the 16th state on **June 1, 1796**, with a condition reducing its initial House representation to one seat.
2. **Early Political Figures and Rivalries:** **John Sevier** became Tennessee's first governor, serving 1796–1801 and again 1803–1809 under the constitution's three-consecutive-term limit, while **William Blount** was elected to the U.S. Senate — only to be expelled in 1797 over a conspiracy to help Britain seize Spanish Florida and Louisiana; he evaded arrest, was welcomed back to Knoxville, won a state senate seat, and died in 1800. Meanwhile, **Andrew Jackson**, elected Tennessee's first U.S. Representative in 1796, developed a bitter rivalry with Sevier over alleged shady land dealings that nearly produced a duel in 1803, even as Jackson's combativeness helped build him a large and loyal following.
3. **Growth and Institution-Building:** The **1800 federal census** recorded roughly 106,000 residents, with a large and growing enslaved population concentrated in Middle Tennessee. The state capital shifted repeatedly — Knoxville, then Nashville (1812), back to Knoxville, then Murfreesboro (1819), and finally Nashville again in 1826 — tracking a broader shift of political power away from East Tennessee. Early institutions like **Blount College** (1794, later the University of Tennessee) and the **Knoxville Gazette** were founded during this period.
4. **Land Cessions and the Opening of West Tennessee:** Continued conflict over land boundaries produced a steady series of treaties, including an 1798 Cherokee cession, culminating in the **1818 Chickasaw Cession** negotiated by Jackson and Kentucky governor Isaac Shelby — the largest single land cession in Tennessee by any tribe, worth $300,000 to the Chickasaw, which opened West Tennessee to settlement and led to the creation of Shelby and Hardin Counties. This steady erosion of Indigenous territory set the stage for the forced removals of the 1830s, a topic reserved for a later lecture.

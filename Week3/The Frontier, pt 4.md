## Summary

### Lecture 4, Part 4: The Frontier — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Labeling the Backcountry Settlers
* **Squatters:** Term applied to Overmountain and backcountry settlers because they occupied land without formal legal claim to it; used literally by British colonial officials in period documents and carried into the early republic.
* **"Crackers":** A disparaging term for backcountry settlers, in use by British officials as early as the 1760s; associated with "great boasters" and rough, disreputable frontier characters, with a disputed etymology tied to "cracking" (boasting) or itinerant "cracking traders."

##### Historiographical Framework
* **Nancy Isenberg (historian):** Referenced for the argument that "squatter" and "cracker" functioned as elite shorthand for landless frontier migrants — a contrast to Thomas Jefferson's ideal of the commercially oriented yeoman cultivator.
* **Thomas Jefferson's Yeoman Ideal:** The vision of the American Republic built on independent, commercially productive landowning farmers, against which backcountry squatters and crackers were unfavorably measured by the political elite.

##### Class Perception & Cultural Legacy
* **Elite Discomfort with Frontier Settlers:** Post-Revolution national elites viewed backcountry people as "rougher around the edges" than the idealized cultivator, associating them with poverty, lack of schooling and church attendance, and social marginality.
* **Formation of Appalachian/Backcountry Identity:** Despite (and partly because of) this disparaging labeling, backcountry settlers developed a distinct regional identity that would later carry real political and cultural weight in 19th-century Tennessee.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This short lecture examines how language and class perception shaped outsiders' views of Tennessee's early backcountry settlers.

1. **"Squatters" and "Crackers" (18th century):** Overmountain and backcountry settlers were routinely labeled **squatters** by British officials because they occupied land without formal legal claim, and increasingly called **"crackers"** — a term British officials used as early as the 1760s for supposedly boastful, rough frontier characters, with a disputed origin in either "cracking" (boasting) or itinerant "cracking traders."
2. **The Jeffersonian Contrast:** Historian **Nancy Isenberg** frames these terms as elite shorthand for landless migrants who fell short of **Thomas Jefferson's** ideal of the commercially oriented yeoman cultivator — backcountry settlers instead lived largely off the grid, rarely attending school or church, which marked them as symbols of poverty and social disorder in the eyes of the new republic's elite.
3. **From Marginalization to Identity:** This gap between elite ideals and backcountry reality generated real discomfort among national political leaders after the Revolution, but it also helped forge a distinct Appalachian/backcountry identity — one that would go on to carry real political and cultural weight in 19th-century Tennessee.

## Summary

### Lecture 4, Part 6: The Frontier — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Origins of the Franklin Movement (1784)
* **North Carolina's Cession of Western Lands (1784):** North Carolina ceded its western land claims (including present-day Tennessee) to the Confederation government under the Articles of Confederation, which encouraged inhabitants of those lands to pursue statehood on their own.
* **Legal Limbo & the "Anarchy" Argument:** Settlers in Upper East Tennessee argued that North Carolina's cession left them without clear governing authority — a state of "anarchy" — while also leaving them exposed to conflict with Indigenous groups, motivating them to seek a new state to restore order and standing.
* **The State of Franklin (proposed 1784):** The new state proposed by Upper East Tennessee settlers, named for Benjamin Franklin; proponents argued a mountain-separated new state would better secure settlers' "lives, liberties, and property" than continued ties to distant North Carolina.

##### Franklin's Contested Government (1784–1787)
* **John Sevier:** Elected governor of the provisional state of Franklin; his leadership of the movement made him, in the professor's paraphrase of historian [Davidson], a man for whom "the times were made... and he was made for them."
* **North Carolina's Reversal:** North Carolina's legislature moved to rescind its cession of western lands soon after the Franklin convention, setting up a direct conflict over which government — Franklin's or North Carolina's — actually had authority in the region.
* **Failed Bid for Congressional Recognition:** Franklin sent representatives to the Confederation Congress (then meeting in New York City) to seek admission as a state, but the effort failed to win approval.

##### Collapse of Franklin (1787–1788)
* **1786 Treaty with the Cherokee:** Sevier, acting as Franklin's governor, negotiated with the Cherokee in 1786, pushing the settlement boundary further south to the Tennessee River.
* **1787 Constitutional Convention:** A second convention failed to produce a new, broadly accepted state constitution, deepening internal opposition to Franklin.
* **John Tipton:** Leader of the pro-North Carolina opposition faction, who orchestrated a scheme leading to Sevier's arrest by North Carolina authorities on charges of treason in 1788.
* **Collapse and Pardon (1788):** North Carolina ended the governorship of Franklin in 1788, pardoned Sevier and other "Franklinites," and Franklin dissolved amid the ensuing chaos — Sevier himself would later be elected to the North Carolina legislature.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Cession Creates a Power Vacuum:** North Carolina's 1784 cession of its western lands — intended to relieve itself of an administrative burden — left Tennessee settlers in genuine uncertainty about who governed them, and that vacuum is what made a homegrown statehood movement possible in the first place.
2. **A State Without a Sponsor:** Franklin operated as a functioning government for several years (treaty-making, elections, a constitution) without ever securing the one thing it actually needed — recognition from North Carolina or the Confederation Congress — which made its eventual collapse structurally inevitable.
3. **Internal Division as the Real Killer:** It wasn't Congress or Native resistance that ended Franklin, but division among the settlers themselves — John Tipton's pro–North Carolina faction directly undermined and ultimately helped topple Sevier's government.
4. **From "Treason" to Reintegration:** Despite Sevier's arrest on treason charges, North Carolina's decision to pardon rather than punish the Franklinites shows how quickly the frontier's political leadership could be reabsorbed into the very authority it had tried to break away from — Sevier himself would go on to serve in North Carolina's legislature before later becoming Tennessee's first governor.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture tells the story of the "Lost State of Franklin," Tennessee's failed 1780s bid for early statehood.

1. **North Carolina's Cession Opens the Door (1784):** Under the Articles of Confederation, North Carolina ceded its western land claims — including present-day Tennessee — to the Confederation government. Settlers in Upper East Tennessee saw this as creating a legal vacuum, reducing them, in their own words, to a state of "anarchy," and combined with ongoing tension with Indigenous groups and the region's geographic separation from North Carolina, it pushed them to organize their own state.
2. **The State of Franklin is Proposed (late 1784):** Proponents held a convention, voted to pursue statehood, named the proposed state Franklin, and elected **John Sevier** governor. Not everyone supported the plan, however — of 43 delegates present, a dissenting faction emerged early, and North Carolina's legislature soon moved to rescind its earlier cession, setting up a direct conflict over legitimate authority in the region.
3. **Franklin Operates Without Recognition (1785–1787):** Franklin functioned as a de facto government for several years — Sevier even negotiated a 1786 treaty with the Cherokee that pushed the settlement boundary south to the Tennessee River — but its representatives failed to win admission from the Confederation Congress in New York, and a 1787 constitutional convention failed to resolve deep internal disagreements about the new state's government.
4. **Collapse and Pardon (1787–1788):** Opposition leader **John Tipton** orchestrated a scheme that led to Sevier's arrest by North Carolina authorities on charges of treason. North Carolina formally ended Franklin's government in 1788 and pardoned Sevier and the other Franklinites rather than prosecuting them. Franklin dissolved amid the chaos of the late 1780s, but the underlying push for Tennessee statehood — the subject of the next lecture — did not end with it.

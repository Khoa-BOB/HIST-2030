# Connections Between Documents 1–3: Suppressing the Atlantic Slave Trade (c. 1819–1823)

## The Documents at a Glance

| Doc | Type | Date | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | U.S. federal statute | Approved March 3, 1819 | "An Act in addition to the act, prohibiting the slave trade" — authorizes the President to send U.S. armed vessels to patrol the African coast, seize American slave ships, pay bounties, and resettle captives in Africa |
| **2** | News item | c. 1822–23 (Congress of Verona era) | Reports that Russia, England, Prussia, and Austria — all but France — agreed at the Congress of Verona to treat the slave trade as piracy, punishable by death, following a memoir by the abolitionist Allen |
| **3** | Newspaper reprint (Baltimore Patriot) | c. 1820 | Reports actual captures of American slave ships (including by the U.S. and by Britain) and accuses Northern merchants of hypocrisy for profiting from the slave trade while denouncing Southern slavery and opposing the extension of slavery into Missouri |

Together the three documents form a **law → enforcement → hypocrisy/critique** sequence: a statute creates the machinery for suppression, a later report shows that machinery becoming an international norm, and a third piece shows how unevenly — and hypocritically — that machinery was actually applied.

## 1. Shared subject: criminalizing and physically stopping the slave trade

All three documents concern the same broader campaign of the early 1820s to treat the transatlantic slave trade as a crime against nations rather than ordinary commerce:

- **Document 1** builds the *legal and operational* apparatus: it lets the President dispatch U.S. Navy and revenue cutters to cruise American coasts, the coast of Africa, "or elsewhere," seize offending U.S. vessels, and prosecute their crews (Sec. 1).
- **Document 2** shows this logic going *international*: by the Congress of Verona, the major European powers (minus France) agreed to classify the slave trade "with the crime of piracy" and punish it with death — the same seize-and-punish framework as Document 1, but escalated and multilateralized.
- **Document 3** shows the *practical result* of both — real ships (the *Rambler*, an unnamed American schooner, and others) actually being captured off West Africa/the Cape Verde Islands.

## 2. Document 1's machinery in action in Document 3

Document 1's specific provisions map directly onto events described in Document 3:

- **Naval seizure (Sec. 1 & 5)**: The Act authorizes armed U.S. vessels to seize American slave ships "on the coast of Africa, or elsewhere." Document 3 reports exactly this happening — American vessels captured near the Cape de Verde Islands, with cargoes of slaves "ordered for Sierra Leone."
- **Bounty system (Sec. 3)**: The Act pays $25 per captive delivered by naval crews. Document 3's emphasis on *who profits* (Northern merchants, "respectable" ship owners) implicitly plays against this bounty/prize-money structure — the Act rewarded interdiction financially, while Document 3 shows the financial incentives on the other side (merchants profiting from the trade itself).
- **African coast agent for resettlement (Sec. 2)**: The Act calls for an agent on the African coast to receive captives freed from seized ships. Document 3's mention of a cargo "ordered for Sierra Leone" (the British colony that served this same resettlement/interdiction role) reflects the same coastal infrastructure the U.S. law anticipated (the U.S. equivalent being the founding of Liberia shortly after).
- **Judicial prosecution (Sec. 4 & Sec. 1 proviso)**: The Act requires prosecuting crews and condemning vessels through the courts. Document 3 cites **Judge Story** addressing a grand jury about New England's involvement in the trade — a direct real-world instance of the judicial enforcement the 1819 Act envisioned.

## 3. Anglo-American cooperation links Documents 2 and 3

Document 3 reports a capture by "his Britannic majesty's brig of war Thistle," i.e., the Royal Navy — not just the U.S. Navy — seizing American-linked slave ships. This is the on-the-ground counterpart to Document 2's report that **England** was among the powers agreeing at Verona to punish the trade as piracy. The two documents together show Britain's anti-slave-trade patrols (the same squadrons that worked alongside/paralleled the U.S. Navy's own patrols under Document 1) actively intercepting American vessels — evidence that Britain was already enforcing the very policy Document 2 says the great powers formally agreed to.

## 4. Escalation over time: seizure/prize law (1819) → piracy/death penalty (1820s international consensus)

Document 1's remedy is essentially a **civil/admiralty one**: seized ships are condemned, proceeds divided as prize money, and crew members merely "proceeded against according to law." Document 2 marks a harsher turn: by the Congress of Verona, participating powers agreed to punish the trade **capitally, as piracy**. Read side by side, the two documents trace the trajectory of anti-slave-trade law in this period — from property seizure and prosecution (U.S., 1819) toward a treaty-level international norm of piracy carrying the death penalty (Europe, early 1820s) — with the U.S. itself moving toward classifying the slave trade as piracy in its own 1820 statute, just after Document 1.

## 5. Document 3 as the critical/ironic counterpoint to Documents 1 and 2

Documents 1 and 2 present the suppression campaign at the level of *official policy* — statutes and international agreements. Document 3 undercuts that official picture by pointing out who was actually still profiting from the trade: **New England merchants**, some of the same region's citizens who publicly opposed extending slavery into Missouri. Its closing biblical jab ("Cast first the beam out thine own eye") frames the entire law-and-treaty apparatus of Documents 1–2 as, in practice, unevenly and hypocritically enforced — the laws existed, ships were sometimes seized (as described in Doc. 1's mechanisms and reported in Doc. 3 itself), yet the same merchant class benefiting from suppression bounties and from moral posturing over Missouri was also secretly bankrolling the trade the law was meant to stop.

## Summary

Read together, Documents 1–3 sketch a single historical arc: the United States legally criminalizes and militarizes suppression of the slave trade in 1819 (**Doc. 1**); the major European powers move toward an even harsher, internationally coordinated piracy standard by the early 1820s (**Doc. 2**); and contemporary press coverage shows both the real enforcement of these rules at sea (American and British naval captures) and the persistent, hypocritical American complicity — especially in the North — that the laws were struggling to stamp out (**Doc. 3**). The Missouri Compromise debate in Document 3 also ties this maritime/international story back to the domestic politics of slavery's expansion that dominated the U.S. in 1819–1820, the same years Document 1 was enacted.

## Research Response

I searched ProQuest Historical Newspapers using the keywords "slave trade" and "slave" to see how a Tennessee paper covered the international campaign against the Atlantic slave trade around the time of the Missouri Compromise. All three results came from the same newspaper, the **Nashville Whig** (Nashville, TN), which ran under that title from 1819 to 1826. Document 1 is the Whig's reprint, under its standing "Laws of the U. States" column, of the federal act "in addition to the act, prohibiting the slave trade," approved March 3, 1819; it authorized the President to send U.S. warships to seize American slave ships and resettle captives in Africa. Document 2, headed "The Slave Trade," is a short, undated dispatch (internal evidence — the Congress of Verona — places it in late 1822 or early 1823) reporting that Russia, England, Prussia, and Austria agreed to treat the trade as piracy, punishable by death. Document 3, "Southern Slave-holding; and Northern Slave-importing," is a Whig reprint (c. 1820) of a Baltimore Patriot article, itself quoting the New-York Columbian and a letter from the American consul at the Cape Verde Islands, naming real captured ships (the *Rambler* and others) and accusing "respectable" Northern merchants of secretly financing the trade.

Together, these clippings show Tennessee readers being kept current on a fast-moving national and international issue at exactly the moment the Missouri crisis was dominating American politics: federal anti-slave-trade enforcement, European powers criminalizing the trade as piracy, and accusations of Northern hypocrisy toward Southern slavery. That all three pieces are reprints of Eastern and European reporting — rather than original Nashville reporting — reflects how a young Tennessee paper built its coverage of national affairs in this period, relying on exchange copies from Baltimore, New York, and Washington. The Whig's choice to reprint the pointedly anti-Northern "beam in thine own eye" piece, published in a slaveholding state during the Missouri debates, also suggests an emerging Southern-sectional editorial slant in Tennessee's early press, one using the hypocrisy of Northern slave-trading merchants to answer Northern moral criticism of Southern slavery.

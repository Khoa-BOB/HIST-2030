## Summary

### Lecture 4, Part 2: The Frontier — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Motivations for Settlement
* **Economic & Legal Push Factors:** Many settlers had been pushed out of their previous homes by financial hardship or legal trouble and sought a fresh start on the frontier.
* **Discontent with North Carolina's Colonial Government:** Resentment over taxation policy and the sense that western settlers were underrepresented, compounded by cultural, social, and religious divides between eastern and western North Carolina.
* **Land Speculation:** Settlers and speculators viewed Cherokee-held land as available for the taking despite existing treaties, hoping to squat first and secure outright ownership later.

##### Treaties, Boundary Lines & Imperial Oversight
* **Proclamation of 1763 (carryover):** The royal prohibition on settlement west of the Appalachians, already being defied as settlers pushed into upper East Tennessee.
* **Treaty of Hard Labor (1768):** Earlier boundary agreement establishing a settlement line between colonists and Cherokee land.
* **Treaty of Lochaber (1770):** A renegotiated treaty line (rendered in the transcript as "the treaty of the Harbor") that opened an additional slice of the region to settlement, though the new boundary remained difficult for settlers to discern or was deliberately disregarded.
* **1771 Boundary Survey:** A formal survey confirming that only the North Holston settlement actually fell within the legally sanctioned settlement line; the other three settlements were found to lie beyond it.
* **Alexander Cameron:** British Indian agent who, based on the survey's findings, declared the settlers at Watauga, Carter's Valley, and Nolichucky to be squatters and ordered them to vacate.

##### The Four Founding Settlements (Upper East Tennessee, ~1768–1769)
* **North Holston:** The first of the four settlements, and the only one later confirmed to be within the legal treaty line.
* **Watauga:** Settlement that became the center of organized settler self-government in the region.
* **Carter's Valley:** One of the three settlements later declared outside the legal boundary.
* **Nolichucky (River):** The fourth settlement, founded along the river of the same name (rendered in the transcript as "Truc river").

##### Settler Self-Government & Cherokee Negotiation
* **Watauga Association:** A self-organized government formed by settlers from the three "squatter" settlements after being ordered to vacate, which negotiated directly with the Cherokee rather than complying with British officials.
* **The Watauga Lease:** An agreement in which the Cherokee permitted settlers to lease the land for a specified term in exchange for goods — described as a kind of "backwards tenancy" that gave settlers a legal-seeming foothold that could develop into eventual possession.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Discontent-Driven Migration:** Economic hardship, political alienation from North Carolina's colonial government, and land speculation combined to push settlers across the Appalachians despite the **Proclamation of 1763**.
2. **Ambiguous Boundaries, Deliberate Disregard:** Successive treaties — **Hard Labor (1768)** and **Lochaber (1770)** — redrew the settlement line, but settlers either misunderstood or knowingly ignored it to occupy Cherokee land anyway.
3. **From Squatters to Self-Government:** When the **1771 survey** exposed most settlements as illegal and **Alexander Cameron** ordered them vacated, settlers responded not by leaving but by forming their own government, the **Watauga Association**, and negotiating directly with the Cherokee.
4. **An Early Precedent for Frontier Self-Rule:** The Watauga Association's independently negotiated lease with the Cherokee is an early example of frontier settlers building quasi-autonomous governance outside official colonial or British control — a pattern that recurs as Tennessee's frontier develops further.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture picks up where Part 1 left off, tracing the founding of Tennessee's first permanent white settlements and the conflicts over land and authority that immediately followed:

1. **First Permanent Settlement and Its Motivations (~1768–1769):** Around 1768–1769, settlers established the first permanent white settlements in upper East Tennessee, moving directly into Cherokee territory. People came west for varied reasons: some had been pushed out of their previous homes by financial or legal trouble and sought a fresh start; others were driven by discontent with North Carolina's colonial government over taxation and underrepresentation, alongside deeper cultural and religious divides between the colony's eastern and western halves; still others were drawn by land speculation, viewing the region as available to claim despite existing Cherokee treaties.
2. **The Four Settlements:** Four distinct settlements emerged in this initial wave: **North Holston**, **Watauga**, **Carter's Valley**, and **Nolichucky**, marking the first sustained white presence in the region.
3. **Treaty Lines and the 1771 Survey:** Settlement here technically defied the **Proclamation of 1763**, though renegotiated boundary treaties — the **Treaty of Hard Labor (1768)** and the **Treaty of Lochaber (1770)** — had opened parts of the region to settlement. The lines these treaties drew were difficult to interpret and often ignored outright. A **1771 survey** determined that only **North Holston** actually fell within the legal settlement boundary; British Indian agent **Alexander Cameron** subsequently declared the settlers at Watauga, Carter's Valley, and Nolichucky to be squatters and ordered them to leave.
4. **The Watauga Association and Self-Government:** Rather than vacating, representatives from the three affected settlements banded together to form the **Watauga Association**, a self-organized frontier government. They negotiated directly with the Cherokee and secured the **Watauga Lease** — permission to lease the land for a set term in exchange for goods. Settlers viewed this "backwards tenancy" as a workaround to official policy, giving them a foothold on the land that could develop into lasting possession, while also establishing an early, quasi-independent form of self-government on the Tennessee frontier.

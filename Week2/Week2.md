# Week 2 Summary & Study Focus

**Big picture:** Week 2 covers roughly 1754–1780, tracing how the Anglo-Cherokee alliance built during the French and Indian War collapsed into open war, and how that collapse opened the door to Tennessee's first permanent white settlement — a frontier built on treaties that were made, broken, and remade almost as fast as settlers could cross the Appalachians, ending with the Cherokee's regional authority badly weakened and settlers fighting (and winning) at Kings Mountain.

### Quick recap
- **Lecture 3 – European Empires and Tennessee History:** Imperial rivalry among Spain, France, and Britain culminates in the French and Indian War (1754–1763), during which the British build Fort Loudoun in Overhill Cherokee territory to secure an alliance. That alliance sours — a British arms embargo and a hostage crisis at Fort Prince George trigger the Anglo-Cherokee War (1758–1761), which ends with Fort Loudoun's fall, a punitive British campaign, and roughly a third of the Cherokee population dead from combat and disease.
- **Lecture 4, pt. 1 – The Frontier:** Opens with settlers and "long hunters" (e.g., Daniel Boone) pushing into Cherokee territory in defiance of the Proclamation of 1763, drawn by the whitetail deerskin trade — a resource as central to Cherokee subsistence and spirituality as it was to the settler economy. Resource competition prompts some of the colonies' earliest game laws.
- **Lecture 4, pt. 2 – The Frontier:** Tennessee's first permanent settlements (North Holston, Watauga, Carter's Valley, Nolichucky) are founded around 1768–1769. A 1771 survey finds only North Holston within the legal treaty line; the other three are declared squatter settlements, so their settlers form the self-governing Watauga Association and negotiate their own long-term land lease directly with the Cherokee.
- **Lecture 4, pt. 3 – The Frontier:** Richard Henderson's 1775 Sycamore Shoals purchase opens Middle Tennessee to settlement over Cherokee objections. The outbreak of the Revolutionary War draws the Cherokee into conflict with Patriot settlers in 1776; peace treaties in 1777 legalize all four Overmountain settlements. The week closes with the Overmountain Men's decisive 1780 victory at the Battle of Kings Mountain, led in part by John Sevier.

### What to focus on
1. **The alliance-to-war arc (Lecture 3):** Understand *why* the Anglo-Cherokee alliance broke down — an arms embargo and hostage-taking, not just random violence — and why historians treat the resulting war as catastrophic (roughly a third of the Cherokee population lost). This sets the tone for everything that follows: Cherokee territorial control is already weakened before permanent settlement even begins.
2. **The repeating treaty → encroachment → conflict → new treaty cycle:** This pattern shows up at least three times this week — Hard Labor (1768) / Lochaber (1770) → settlers ignore the line → Watauga lease; and Sycamore Shoals (1775) → 1776 war → 1777 treaties. Recognizing this as a *pattern*, not a series of unrelated events, is the single most useful thing to take from the week.
3. **What actually drove settlers west:** Not just "opportunity" in the abstract — specific, nameable forces: the deerskin trade, land speculation (Richard Henderson's Transylvania Company being the biggest example), and real political/economic discontent with North Carolina's colonial government.
4. **The Watauga Association as an early precedent:** Settlers organizing their own government and negotiating directly with the Cherokee — independent of British or colonial authority — is worth remembering as an early seed of frontier self-rule that recurs later in Tennessee's path to statehood.
5. **Key names to anchor the narrative:** Cherokee leaders **Attakullakulla**, **Oconostota**, and **Dragging Canoe** (representing different Cherokee responses to settler encroachment); frontier/colonial figures **Richard Henderson**, **Alexander Cameron**, **Patrick Henry**, and **John Sevier**; and historians **Daniel Tortora** and **Drew Swanson**, whose framing (catastrophic loss; deer as both economy and cosmology) does a lot of the analytical work in these lectures.
6. **Anchor dates:** 1754–1763 (French and Indian War), 1756–1761 (Fort Loudoun built, then falls), 1763 (Proclamation), 1768–1771 (first settlements and the survey that exposes most of them as illegal), 1775 (Sycamore Shoals), 1776–1777 (Cherokee-settler war and peace treaties), 1780 (Kings Mountain).

## Summary

### Lecture 36: The Yellow Fever Epidemic of 1878 — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### The Disease
* **Yellow Fever:** A virus in the same group as West Nile, also called **Yellow Jack**, the **saffron scourge**, and the **American plague**. It cannot spread through air or touch and depends entirely on mosquitoes. Symptoms included high fever, black vomit, hemorrhage, and delirium.
* **Aedes aegypti:** The striped mosquito that was especially good at spreading yellow fever in America. *[Transcript: "Aile," "aid of Egypt die," "a Jedi."]*
* **The Atlantic World Origins:** Yellow fever began in Africa and crossed the Atlantic by ship with European expansion and the slave trade. The first Western Hemisphere epidemic came in **1648**. Land transformed for rice, indigo, and cotton gave mosquitoes an ideal environment.
* **North-to-South Shift:** Northern cities suffered while the Atlantic slave trade lasted (until roughly **1807**). After that, most yellow fever occurred in Southern states.

##### Why Memphis?
* **Transportation Networks:** Memphis was a river town on the Mississippi, tied to New Orleans and the Gulf, and connected to railroads by the 1850s. Infected people traveled along these routes.
* **El Niño Cycle (1878):** A mild, warm winter and unusually high rainfall made the mosquito population explode. People did not understand this at the time.
* **Non-Immune Newcomers:** European immigrants and other new arrivals had not had yellow fever, so they had no immunity.
* **Growing Population:** Immigrants, freed people, and poor white people moved to the city. Crosby gives a **1870** population of **40,000**, almost double Nashville and Atlanta, and calls Memphis second only to New Orleans as the largest city in the South. *[Correction: Memphis's 40,226 was about 1.5 times Nashville's 25,865. Richmond (51,038) and Charleston (48,956) were also larger in the 1870 census, so "second largest" does not hold.]*
* **Failed City Government and Filth:** Memphis was a violent river town, later called the "murder capital of America." Its corrupt, debt-ridden government did not invest in infrastructure. The city had no modern waterworks or sewage removal, and it had stagnant pools, sewage, dead animals, and manure in the streets.
* **"Sick City" Reputation:** Memphis had already had two yellow fever epidemics plus cholera and malaria outbreaks. *[Correction: sources record at least three earlier yellow fever outbreaks, in 1855, 1867, and 1873.]*

##### Key Figures & Groups
* **Molly Caldwell Crosby:** Author of *The American Plague*, the book this lecture relies on most.
* **Sisters of St. Mary's:** Episcopal sisters who stayed to feed and comfort the sick and to care for orphans, including African American children. They are among the **Martyrs of Memphis**, along with ministers, doctors, and priests.
* **Annie Cook:** A brothel owner who turned her **Mansion House** into a hospital and nursed the sick until the fever killed her.
* **Dr. Carlos Finlay:** A Cuban doctor who studied the striped mosquito as the vector in the 1880s and presented his theory to the Royal Academy in **1881**. His stutter and only partly successful experiments helped others dismiss him as the "mosquito man."
* **Major Walter Reed:** A U.S. Army doctor who is usually credited with solving the mystery. His associates, who spent more time in Cuba than he did, sought out Finlay and experimented on themselves and healthy volunteers. *[Transcript: "Walter Reid."]*

##### Major Events & Consequences
* **Cuba Connection and Weakened Quarantine:** Ships and refugees came from Cuba at the end of the **Ten Years' War**. New Orleans business interests pushed to weaken quarantine, and yellow fever gained a foothold there by the end of May 1878.
* **Mass Exodus:** More than **25,000** Memphians, over half the population, fled. Most had the means to leave. Of about **47,000** residents in July, **19,000** remained by September, and **17,000** of them caught the fever.
* **Loss of the Charter:** Memphis lost its charter on the last day of 1878 and became a **taxing district** of the state until **1893**. *[Correction: the legislature passed the repeal at the end of January 1879.]*

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Global trade carried the disease:** The slave trade, Atlantic commerce, and later river and rail networks moved both the virus and the mosquitoes.
2. **Environment and ignorance:** People changed the land and left stagnant water, creating mosquito habitat, while no one knew the mosquito was the vector.
3. **Government failure:** Corruption, debt, and a late **$8,000** to the Board of Health left Memphis unprepared. New Orleans business interests also put commerce ahead of quarantine.
4. **Class shaped survival:** People with means fled, while those without means stayed behind and got sick. Many who left never came back, which changed the city.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture explains what yellow fever is, why the **1878** epidemic struck Memphis so hard, and how the mosquito was finally identified as the cause. The lecturer says **"Why Memphis?"** will be a question on the **final exam**.

1. **The Long History (1648–1800s):** **Yellow fever** came from Africa on ships, spread by trade and the **slave trade**, and caused its first Western Hemisphere epidemic in **1648**. Crop agriculture reshaped the land into an ideal home for **Aedes aegypti**. Outbreaks recurred for more than 200 years, and no one knew how the disease spread until about **1900**. After the Atlantic slave trade ended around **1807**, the outbreaks moved mainly to the South.
2. **Why Memphis (1870s):** Three big factors were **transportation networks**, an **El Niño** winter, and **non-immune immigrants**. Memphis itself was growing quickly, and Crosby ranks it second only to New Orleans in the South. *[Correction: Richmond and Charleston were also larger in 1870.]* It was also violent, run by a corrupt government, and deep in debt. It had no modern water or sewage system, so stagnant water, sewage, and manure sat in the streets. People blamed filth or shipping and railroads, and the Board of Health received only **$8,000** in 1878, far too late.
3. **The Outbreak (May–August 1878):** Debate over **quarantine** was already ongoing. Ships and refugees from **Cuba**, at the end of the **Ten Years' War**, reached New Orleans, where business interests had weakened quarantine. Confirmation of the disease in New Orleans came too late, since ships had already gone upriver. In Memphis, one of the first victims arrived by riverboat on **July 21**. The first case was reported only in **August**, and the Board of Health declared an epidemic on **August 23**.
4. **Inside the Epidemic (Aug.–Dec. 1878):** More than **25,000** people fled, leaving **19,000**, of whom **17,000** caught the fever. The **Sisters of St. Mary's**, doctors, clergy, and **Annie Cook** stayed to give care. Doctors saw 100 to 150 patients a day with quinine, morphine, castor oil, laudanum, and iced champagne, and some treatments, like sitting over burning rum, did more harm than good. The epidemic was declared over in **December**. *[Correction: sources say it ended with the first frost in late October; I could not confirm a December declaration.]*
5. **Aftermath and the Solution (1878–c. 1900 and after):** Memphis lost its charter on the last day of 1878 and stayed a **taxing district** until **1893**. *[Correction: the repeal passed at the end of January 1879.]* Many wealthy residents never returned. **Carlos Finlay** proposed the mosquito theory in **1881** but was dismissed. After the U.S. military presence in Cuba grew around the **Spanish-American War**, **Walter Reed** and his associates used Finlay's expertise and human experiments to confirm the link. Later came an understanding of viruses, a vaccine, and continued mosquito control.

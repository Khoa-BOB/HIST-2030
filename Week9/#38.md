## Summary

### Lecture 38: Progressive Era Reform & the Fight for Prohibition — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Movements & Organizations
* **Progressive Era Reform:** A movement based largely in Tennessee's upper middle and upper classes. Its two big causes were statewide **prohibition** and **women's suffrage**.
* **Tennessee Temperance Alliance and WCTU:** Groups formed in the **1880s** to push for a statewide constitutional amendment banning alcohol sales. The amendment failed.
* **Wets and Dries:** The two camps that divided Tennessee politics. Opponents of prohibition were concentrated in the cities, with Knoxville as the exception.

##### Laws
* **Four Mile Law:** Barred saloons within 4 miles of schools. Prohibitionists used it to dry up the state county by county after the statewide effort failed.
* **1909 Manufacturing Ban:** A law against making alcoholic beverages in the state. The legislature overrode Governor **Malcolm Patterson's** veto.
* **Ouster Law:** Allowed removal of local officials who did not enforce the prohibition laws. *[Transcript: "Auster law." Unconfirmed: the lecture ties it to Gov. Rye, while one source credits Rye's predecessor, Gov. Ben Hooper, with pushing the 1915 law.]*
* **18th Amendment:** Banned the manufacture and sale of alcohol nationwide. Tennessee was technically dry about a decade earlier.

##### Key Figures
* **E. H. Crump:** Elected Memphis mayor in 1910, he openly defied prohibition because his Black and white constituents opposed it. He still confined the red-light district. After his removal, he built his machine as a city trustee. *[Corrections: he was elected in November 1909 and took office in 1910. The post was Shelby County Trustee, a county office.]*
* **Hilary Howse:** The Nashville mayor who said, in paraphrase, that he did not shut down saloons but patronized them. He was also removed from office. *[Transcript: "Hillary Howe," "Hilary house."]*
* **Thomas Rye:** A Democratic prohibitionist elected governor in **1914**.

##### Effects
* **Underground Alcohol:** Liquor stayed available. Distilleries like **Jack Daniel** had to relocate out of state, and moonshining continued.
* **Memphis Red-Light District:** Closed entirely in **1917** to lure a military base, today located in Millington, as the U.S. entered World War I.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Prohibition and disfranchisement were linked:** Dries unfairly blamed Black voters for their defeat, which supported efforts to restrict Black men's votes, although some Black Tennesseans belonged to temperance groups.
2. **Rural vs. urban:** Small towns went dry while Memphis, Nashville, and Chattanooga resisted.
3. **Law vs. enforcement:** Each act of mayoral defiance pushed prohibitionists toward a tougher tool, ending with removal from office.
4. **Issue alliances:** Urban mayors and the liquor industry opposed both prohibition and suffrage because they associated women's suffrage with prohibition.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture follows the fight over prohibition, the first of two major Progressive Era causes in Tennessee.

1. **Early Campaign (1880s):** The **Tennessee Temperance Alliance** and the **WCTU** pushed for a constitutional amendment. It failed, and supporters were mocked as "well-meaning but sentimental women and three-dollar preachers." Prohibitionists blamed the "Black vote," which was unfair and helped justify voter restrictions.
2. **Wets, Dries, and County-by-County Drying:** The issue split politics in two. Small towns went dry with the **Four Mile Law**, and Knoxville voted itself dry, while Memphis, Nashville, and Chattanooga resisted. In **1909**, the legislature banned alcohol manufacturing over Governor **Patterson's** veto.
3. **Urban Defiance:** **Hilary Howse** of Nashville and **E. H. Crump** of Memphis refused to enforce the laws because they knew their constituents wanted to drink. *[Correction: Crump was elected in 1909, not 1910.]* Illegal liquor stayed available, **Jack Daniel** moved out of state, and moonshine continued.
4. **The Prohibitionists Strike Back (1914–1917):** Governor **Thomas Rye**, elected in **1914**, backed the **Ouster Law**, and both Crump and Howse were removed. Crump then built his machine from behind the scenes as a city trustee. *[Correction: Shelby County Trustee.]* Tougher laws followed in **1917**, the same year Memphis closed its **red-light district** to attract a military base. It was a temporary victory, since people kept drinking.

### Lecture 37: Tennessee in the New South, 1880s–1920 — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### The New South Idea
* **New South:** Mostly a **PR slogan** used by promoters who wanted a modern, industrial South. There were still many lines of continuity with the pre-war South.
* **Tennessee Centennial (1897):** An exposition that looked to both the future and the past, embodied by the replica **Parthenon** in Nashville. It shows the irony of promoters who also romanticized the antebellum era and the ancient past.

##### Economy & Labor
* **Sharecropping / Tenant Farming:** The system that trapped many non-landowning farmers after slavery ended. It was very hard to escape.
* **Manufacturing and Extraction:** The state stayed mostly agricultural but added iron foundries, flour milling, coal mining, and timber, with manufacturing notably in Knoxville and Chattanooga. Workers often earned subsistence wages.
* **Convict Lease Labor:** The state leased out people in prison to companies such as mines. Companies saw it as a bargain compared with paying wage labor.
* **Coal Miner War (1891–1893):** East Tennessee coal miners fought a guerrilla war against companies replacing free wage labor with convict lease labor. *[Correction: usually called the Coal Creek War and dated 1891–1892.]*
* **Railroads:** They expanded quickly after the Civil War to thousands of miles by about 1900. They brought prosperity and ended isolation, but they also drew criticism for corruption, political scandals, and economic dominance.
* **Joel Cheek:** A New South entrepreneur who marketed the premium **Maxwell House** coffee in the 1890s, named after a Nashville hotel.

##### Politics & Jim Crow
* **Party System:** Democrats were fairly dominant and the **Populists** challenged them. Unusually for the South, Republicans could still win elections in Tennessee.
* **Jim Crow Laws:** Efforts to mandate segregated schools and ban interracial marriage began in the 1870s. In **1881**, the legislature required separate railroad coaches.
* **Voter Restrictions:** Starting eight years later, lawmakers used measures that looked race-blind to get around the **15th Amendment**: advanced registration, the **poll tax**, and the **secret ballot**, which especially hurt illiterate voters. Poor white voters lost out too.
* **E. H. Crump:** The Memphis political boss who rose in the 1910s when elected mayor. His machine kept Black men voting in Memphis, which made Tennessee an exception in the South. *[Transcript: "Saint Edward Crunk." Correction: he was elected in November 1909 and took office in 1910.]*

##### Racial Violence & Reformers
* **Lynching:** Racialized mob violence that peaked in the 1890s. Victims could be accused of even minor "offenses," such as not tipping a hat, or targeted for simply being too successful.
* **Ida B. Wells:** Born into slavery and later based in Memphis, she wrote against lynching. After the **People's Grocery lynching (1892)**, threats to her life forced her to leave, and she continued her advocacy in exile.
* **Mary Church Terrell:** From Memphis's prominent Church family, she founded the **National Association of Colored Women** and was active in the suffrage movement.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Modern slogan, old patterns:** Promoters advertised a new region while agriculture, tenancy, and romantic views of the past continued.
2. **Cheap labor produced conflict:** Low wages and convict leasing pushed workers to protests, strikes, and guerrilla war.
3. **Race-blind on paper:** The poll tax and similar rules got around the 15th Amendment and reduced political participation among both Black and poor white voters.
4. **The Tennessee exception:** Surviving Republicans and urban bosses like **Crump** meant that Black men in Tennessee never entirely lost the vote.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture hits the high points of Tennessee's economy, politics, and racial order across the 40 years of the New South.

1. **The New South Idea:** The **New South** was mainly a promotional slogan for modernizing and industrializing. White Tennesseans also looked backward, which the **1897 Centennial** and its replica **Parthenon** show clearly.
2. **Economy and Labor:** Tennessee stayed largely agricultural, with many farmers caught in **sharecropping**. Iron, flour milling, coal, and timber grew, but workers often earned only subsistence wages. East Tennessee miners fought the **Coal Miner War (1891–1893)** against **convict lease labor**. *[Correction: the Coal Creek War, 1891–1892.]* **Railroads** spread rapidly and drew both welcome and suspicion, and entrepreneurs like **Joel Cheek** sold products nationwide.
3. **Politics and Disfranchisement:** Reform, schools, roads, and urban vice were the big issues. Democrats dominated, but Republicans could still win. **Jim Crow** laws expanded, including separate railroad coaches in **1881**, and the **poll tax**, advanced registration, and **secret ballot** followed about eight years later. Unlike in states such as Mississippi, Black men in Tennessee never entirely lost the vote, partly because bosses like **E. H. Crump** relied on Black voters. *[Correction: Crump was elected mayor in 1909, not the 1910s, and took office in 1910.]*
4. **Violence and Resistance:** **Lynching** peaked in the 1890s even as Klan activity subsided. **Ida B. Wells** spoke out, and the **People's Grocery lynching (1892)** led to threats that forced her out of Memphis. She and **Mary Church Terrell** became prominent national voices, connecting to the civil rights and suffrage movements.

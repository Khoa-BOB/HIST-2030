# CIVIL WAR THREATENED: Menacing Aspect of the Quarrel Between the President and Congress

**Source:** *Republican Banner* (Nashville), Oct 2, 1867, pg. 1. ProQuest Historical Newspapers: The Nashville Tennessean.

## CIVIL WAR THREATENED.

### Menacing Aspect of the Quarrel Between the President and Congress—Probable Resistance of the Former to any Ursurpation of his Perogatives—Animus of the Northern People.

*(From the Cincinnati Commercial (Rad.), Sept. 23.)*

In the discharge of our duty as journalists we have, within a few days, called the attention of the public to the facts showing the existence of serious danger that the President and Congress will consummate their quarrel in a civil war. In the *Commercial*, issued on Friday last, we pointed out the probable ground of disputed authority that might produce an armed collision. It was, that in case of impeachment there would be a movement by extreme men to at once suspend the President from the functions of his office, and that he would resist such an attempt. The President is contemplating resistance to Congress in a certain state of facts. It follows that he is taking measures to make his resistance effective—that he is making preparations for war with Congress, provided that body does that which he considers unconstitutional. It is certain, in our judgment, that he is deceiving himself as to the sentiments of the people and the chances of war, and that it is important he should be undeceived. If the President could feel an assurance of popular support, we have no doubt he would refuse to submit to trial by the Senate. Granting that he may interpret the Constitution as he pleases, and construe the laws at pleasure, he can easily deny that the Senate, as constituted, is a body competent to try him. The favorite phrase of his personal organ, the *National Intelligencer*, is, that if he is tried at all, it must be by a "full Senate." That is, that the Southern States must all be represented before the Senate can be recognized as a tribunal equal to the responsibility of trying the President on an indictment found by the House. Then it has been repeatedly stated by the President's organs that Senators who have committed themselves on the question of his guilt should not take part in his trial. So, the more this question is examined the greater seem the chances for a collision. If it appears, when Congress assembles, that the President is preparing to resist the will of the people as expressed through that body, it would be necessary to take precautionary measures, securing success if not peace, and there is no telling just where the President would show fight.

That Congress may be rash and indiscreet, we recognize as quite within the limits of possibility, but the danger to be apprehended from the impetuosity of two hundred men is, by no means, so great as that to be looked for in the headlong passion of one man. The question of impeachment, we have not at any time forgotten, is one of extreme gravity. It should be approached, not in a partisan, but a judicial spirit. And we would further consider not whether the President deserves impeachment, but whether it would be profitable to the country to impeach him. If the President does not, before the meeting of Congress, commit some overt act in the obstruction of the laws, or menacing the authority of Congress, it is our strong impression that it would not pay to be at the trouble to impeach him. Considering the amount of public business demanding immediate attention, the fact that in a little more than a year the term of office of the President will expire, and that he would then forever cease from troubling; and the fact that during the remainder of his term Congress will be nearly all the time in session, and has ample power to make and unmake laws irrespective of the Executive veto—considering all these things, it is our judgment that Congress could be better employed than in impeaching the President. If impeachment is undertaken, we hope Congress will not assume the exercise of doubtful powers—that it will conduct the whole proceeding so that if the President makes resistance the case against him may be perfectly clear.

On one subject we have no doubts. Congress will, in any event, be sustained by the party that was the National War party in the struggle between the Government and the rebellion. If there is a call to arms, the million soldiers disbanded after the surrender of the rebel armies will enlist for the new war as rapidly as they were mustered out of service, and there is no power within call of Andrew Johnson that could stand before them. The authorities in the Northern States, and the victorious veterans of the late war, would march all one way, and speedily dispose of all opposition.

*(From the Cincinnati Enquirer (Dem.), Sept. 25th.)*

Congress has the constitutional right to impeach the President for treason, high crimes and misdemeanors. President Johnson is afraid of no such indictment, well knowing that it cannot be sustained. Indeed, he courts the investigation such accusation must produce into his private and public conduct. So far, there is, and need be, no apprehension of violence. The point of danger is here: Will the recommendation of Wendell Phillips and Gen. Ben. Butler, to suspend Mr. Johnson from office until the Senate shall have given its decision on the impeachment, be attempted to be carried out?

What does the *Commercial* say? Does it approve of that programme? And if it does not, what does it say the President should do if an attempt should be made to depose him from his office before the Senate shall have disposed of his impeachment? Should he resist the usurpation or should he submit? All the poppy-cock talk of the *Commercial*, the blood and thunder of the St. Louis *Democrat*, and terror of Senators Thayer and Howard, all center in that one point.

If an attempt shall be made to oust Mr. Johnson from office, in case of impeachment, before the Senate shall have given its verdict, *he will resist the attempt with all the power he can command.* That is the long and the short of all the attempted sensation about the President going to seize and imprison Congress. He will resist usurpation of his prerogatives by force, if necessary. He has as much right to do that as he has to exercise the functions of the Presidency, which right cannot be disputed by sane persons.

*(From the N. Y. World (Dem.), Sept. 25th.)*

From our knowledge of the temper of the Democratic party, we have no hesitation in saying that it will face the emergency with as much vigor and intrepidity as indignant human nature is very well capable of. It is our clear and decided judgment that influential Democrats ought not to go into the Cabinet or except any office under the President, and thus expose the party, in the great struggle which may come, to the imputation of sordid motives. We can easily convict the impeachers of such motives, but the charge would loose its effect if, with any color of truth, it could be retorted. If it were merely a fight between Congress and the President, we should have no interest except as bystanders to see fair play. But it is of far higher consequence. It is an attempt, by one branch of the government, to destroy the independence, and subvert the constitutional rights of the other.

To put out a President for the base motive of getting control of the Federal patronage for electioneering purposes, would be a decree of political degeneracy not a whit better than the *pronunciamentos* and anarchy which have so long afflicted Mexico. If President Johnson is impeached and deposed for no crime but fidelity to the Constitution, and the people submit to it, the country will have reached the lowest stage of political degradation.

If, unhappily, it should come to a conflict of force, it will be for us to decide, on high public grounds, free from any identification with the personal fortunes of Mr. Johnson, where duty might call us in so serious a conjecture. The controversy will pass through many phases before it reaches the stage at which that question can arise; and Democrats, while shrinking from no responsibility which public duty may require of them, hope to defeat the impeachment project by other methods than a resort to force. Whenever it becomes necessary, they will cause it to be fully understood that they will stand resolutely—not by the President, who is nothing to them—but by the Constitution threatened to be outraged in his person. They will make no sacrifices for the man, but they will shrink from no sacrifice which may become necessary to vindicate the rights of the office.

## A New and Valuable Plant.

*(Separate short item printed in the same column.)*

The Charleston (S. C.) *Courier* has an account of a wonderful volunteer tomato plant, which was produced in the little town of Waldo, Alachua county, Florida, the present season. It covers a bed eighteen feet square. This remarkable growth was produced by the same habit by which the banyan tree multiplies itself indefinitely. The top of the plant became so heavy that it bent to the ground, and the branches taking root, other new plants grew up, and these again produced others, each new sprout producing fruit, the same as the original plant. This process has gone on until the space covered measures, as we have said, eighteen feet square. The first fruit produced was in the latter part of May, and the vine has been known to have as much as a bushel of perfect fruit upon it at one time. By the latter part of August it had produced about six bushels, and was still giving a good supply. The family to whom it belonged was supplied entirely from the one vine, besides giving away large quantities. It is evidently a new variety of this valuable garden vegetable.

# TN History in the Archives: Assignment #3

**Database:** ProQuest Historical Newspapers: The Nashville Tennessean
**Search term:** "civil war"
**Sources:**
1. "Civil War: Steps Taken by the Radical Leaders for the Conflict," *Republican Banner* (Nashville), November 1, 1866, p. 1.
2. "Civil War Threatened: Menacing Aspect of the Quarrel Between the President and Congress," *Republican Banner* (Nashville), October 2, 1867, p. 1.

---

A search for the term "civil war" in ProQuest's historical Nashville newspapers yields not only accounts of the conflict of 1861–1865 but also warnings of a second one. Two front-page articles from the Nashville *Republican Banner*, published in 1866 and 1867, demonstrate that the Civil War did not end for Tennesseans at Appomattox. Instead, its divisions persisted into Reconstruction and centered on a Tennessean, President Andrew Johnson.

The first article, published on November 1, 1866, reprints correspondence from the New York *World* alleging that Radical Republicans were preparing for "another civil war" against the President. The author cites a House resolution arming state militias, under which weapons would reach the "loyal" states immediately while former Confederate states such as Tennessee would wait "until further orders." This provision reveals that the national government continued to distrust Tennessee after the war. The article also records Radical threats of impeachment, including one speaker's declaration that the nation possessed "an axe" for Johnson's "official head." Quoting Henry Raymond, the author warns that such a conflict "would be a war of political parties and of neighborhoods," an image that reflected the wartime experience of Tennessee civilians, whose state had been divided between Unionist East Tennessee and Confederate Middle and West Tennessee.

The second article, published on October 2, 1867, compiles Northern editorials on the impeachment controversy. The Radical Cincinnati *Commercial* predicts that "the million soldiers disbanded" would re-enlist against Johnson, whereas the Democratic Cincinnati *Enquirer* insists that the President would resist removal "with all the power he can command." These competing claims show that the veterans of the war remained a political force and that Tennessee's internal divisions now shaped national politics, with Johnson, a Tennessee Unionist, positioned against the victorious North.

In conclusion, the *Republican Banner*'s decision to feature these warnings on its front page indicates that its readers continued to live with the fear and division the Civil War had produced. Both articles show that the conflict's effects on Tennessee extended well beyond the battlefield. For many Tennesseans, the peace that followed 1865 remained fragile, and the possibility of renewed conflict remained real.

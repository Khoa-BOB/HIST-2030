## CIVIL WAR.

### Steps Taken by the Radical Leaders for the Conflict—Organization and Manipulation of the Militia—Impeachment and Guillotine Speeches of Radical Chiefs, Etc.

*Correspondence of the New York World.*

WASHINGTON, Oct. 28.

The bold threats of resistance and civil war by the Radicals, in connection with the Baltimore Police question, is no sudden thought on the part of their leaders. They have been preparing for war for several months. Their preparations have been made quietly, and in the same manner that the secession leaders of the South prepared for war prior to the election of 1860. The evidence on this point is conclusive, and the coincidence between the course which the Northern Radicals are pursuing now, and that of the secessionists before the rebellion, is certainly remarkable. If your readers will but recall the events of the last nine months they will see how close the parallel is. If they are allowed to go unrebuked by the people throughout the North the same result, namely, civil war, may follow.

The Radicals commenced by declaring that the President's acts in any other country would have cost him his head. They heaped abuse upon abuse against him, and indulged in language such as was never uttered in the halls of Congress against a public officer. Thus matters went on, every possible means being resorted to for the purpose of inflaming the minds of the people. Inquisitorial committees published ex parte statements, until, finally, just before the close of Congress, a proposition for the several States to arm their militia was pushed through Congress, and would not have attracted any particular attention had it not been for the exposure of Raymond. Here is what he said of it in his famous letter in July last:

> "You may have noticed the passage in the House a few days since, of a resolution offered by Gen. Paine, of Wisconsin, calling on the State to organize, discipline, and equip their militia, and directing that two-thirds of the arms, ordnance and ammunition now under custody of the General Government be distributed among the States—the distribution among the loyal States to take place immediately, and that among the States lately in rebellion to be postponed until further orders. The resolution came from the Committee on Military Affairs, and was pushed to a vote, without debate or delay under the previous question. It attracted as little attention in Congress as it has in the country; and the public will doubtless receive with incredulity the assurance that it was intended, by those who secured its passage, as the first step toward preparation for another civil war.
>
> "Although no debate was had upon it, members were urged to vote for it by direct conversational appeals on the floor on the part of the few who were privy to its introduction. Some were told that it was necessary to enable the Southern loyalists to protect themselves; others that it was simply a matter of detail in the War Department; others that the arms must be taken out of the hands of the President; and others that it was proposed at the instance of the Secretary of War. An appeal was made by Mr. Kasson, of Iowa, to allow debate upon it, as it seemed to be a matter of importance, but it was refused."

That this was one of the first steps toward civil war was evident from the speech of Boutwell and others in the Congressional caucus at the time over the question whether they should remain in session or not. Mr. Boutwell, in his remarks on the militia resolution, stated that,

> "It was the determination to arouse the North, and prepare for a resort to force upon the assembling of the Fortieth Congress in extra or in regular session; and this determination is avowed. And the resolution to which I have referred, for an organization of the militia and a distribution of arms in the Northern States, is the initial step to its execution."

In connection with this and the developments in the Congressional caucus Mr. Raymond in his letter truthfully stated:

> "I do not propose to comment upon the result of such a movement. It is obvious that if any such contingency should arise, the war would not be sectional, as was the last; it would be a war of political parties and of neighborhoods. Not only have the great body of the Union party in Congress no sympathy with these views and purposes, but they are in the main ignorant and incredulous of their existence. That the extreme Radicals entertain them, however, there is not the slightest doubt, and we know, from the experience of secession in 1861, how few men it sometimes requires to plunge a great party or a great nation into war."

Soon after this Congress adjourned the Radical leaders commenced their harangue at home. Wendell Phillips, quick to scent the movements of the fanatical faction, declared that President Johnson was an obstacle in the way, and must be deposed. A short time elapsed, when it was announced that General Butler had been appointed Commander-in-Chief of the Militia of Massachusetts.

As the campaign in the Central States progressed, the Radical leaders became more violent. It was manifested by the refusal of city government in Philadelphia to receive the President as their guest, and then again by the Radical Senators in Albany.

Soon after this, Bingham, a Radical member of Congress from Ohio, declared in a speech that

> "So help me God, I will neither give sleep to my eyes, nor slumber to my eyelids, until I shall have drawn bills of impeachment against Andrew Johnson."

The next development was the announcement that Governor Fenton had commenced removing the Conservative commanders of the militia in your State, and was appointing loyal leaguers in their places. In those cases where he could not find an excuse for removal of commanders of brigades, he has transferred regiments from one brigade to another, in order to enable him to designate a loyal leaguer to command the brigade.

Then came the famous impeachment speech of General Butler, in which he read his specification, or bill of indictment, as follows:

> "1. Attempt to bring Congress into public hatred, ridicule, and contempt.
>
> "2. Wrongfully using the power of appointment and disregarding the prerogatives of the United States Senate in the appointment of men whom that body, in pursuance of constitutional authority, had tried and rejected.
>
> "3. For declaring peace in the insurrectionary States without the consent of Congress.
>
> "4. For corruptly using the pardoning power.
>
> "5. For failing to enforce the Civil Rights Bill.
>
> "6. For complicity in the New Orleans riot.
>
> "Stevens and Boutwell and Schenck will be among the foremost in this business, while a dozen leading Senators have already committed themselves in favor of the arraignment of the President."

At a subsequent speech in Chicago he added one more specification to these charges:

> His appointment to office in the South of men who do not take the test oath.

In his Cincinnati speech he told the public how the impeachment would be prosecuted, and then hinted that an army was armed and equipped ready to enforce the Radical decree. Here are his own words on this point:

> "We are told that if Congress shall proceed to exercise their rightful authority, then there will be tried the strength of the Government, that the President will call upon the army and navy, and the army and navy will obey him. Let there be no fear about that, because the army and navy of the United States are not those few men that are in the regular service. (Applause.) I have no desire to disparage either the patriotism or integrity of the army of the United States, but if the army, as such, or if any portion of it, or if any officer of it, shall so far forget the duties he owes to his flag and to his profession as a soldier as to answer any but the legal call of his country, that small body of men shall be swept from the face of the earth as a cloud is swept away before the rising of the morning sun. (Prolonged applause.)"

As soon as the October elections were over, other Radical orators spoke out; among them, Shellabarger, of Ohio, in the following style:

> "And let it be understood by all whom it may concern, that Tuesday's elections meant that while this nation has a mantle for a fault of inauguration day, and a Lethe for him who merely outrages all the proprieties of official station and of private life, yet for him who presumes to touch the sacred life of the Government this nation has an axe. And let me, in all calmness and charity, say to the President, that Tuesday's elections signify that should one step be taken by him to put into execution that threat that the Congress is to be ignored, then speedily, relentlessly, on his official head, that axe will fall. And then a bad President will be remembered as eminent only as a national dishonor."

Thus from impeachment came the cry for the axe. Wendell Phillips again turned up at this stage, and advocated that Congress should make itself a perpetual body, and commence its session and not adjourn.

> "He would say to the New York Tribune, the National Republican Committee, and Congress itself: 'Go back and sit down in the House of Representatives, and govern the Republic; and for the first act of that Government, impeach the President.' (Prolonged applause.) He would have the House of Representatives impeach the President, place the President of the Senate in his office, and then the people would run the machine. (Applause.) His theory, Mr. Phillips said, was that there is no President. He is a deserter. The legislative power is the only power felt. Our James II has abdicated, and Parliament rules the nation. He wanted Congress to commence its next session as a perpetual one. Every moment that it was not in session the South was ruling the Government; and he would have the next Congress enact that they should re-assemble in March, 1867, and be themselves the Government."

The Worcester *Spy*, a violent Radical sheet in Massachusetts, then came out and asked what is to be done with the victory. It answered its own question in the following revolutionary language:

> "The logic of the election is that the President must retire from his position, and leave the whole question of restoring the lately insurgent States to the Union to the judgment of the loyal people thereof, expressed through their constitutional Representatives in Congress. We already hear from various sources that leading members of the Administration hope to make their peace with the people they have betrayed by opposing the further use of public patronage for purposes of corruption. It is also hinted that some of them will make a show of supporting the Constitutional Amendment in the hope of thus escaping the purifying storm which these elections foreshadow. It does not appear that any of these motives of discretion have yet shaken the purpose or subdued the will of the incarnate treachery of the Head of the Government. If they had, it would not be enough. The country demands that this stumbling-block in the way of a just and speedy reconstruction shall be at once removed."

It was soon whispered around among the Radicals that General Logan had a force of 25,000 men organized and equipped, ready to march on Washington, and that preparations had been made to increase his force as he marched eastward until it numbered 200,000. It was not many days before it came out that the Governors of some of the Western States had acted together in receiving arms and placing them in the hands of their followers. Yet no particular attention was paid to all this until Forney came out with his blood and thunder letter about the affairs in Baltimore, declaring that the Radicals then were ready and determined to fight to the bitter end. The Cincinnati *Gazette* made the announcement at this juncture that, had the elections gone against the Radicals, the result would have been resisted.

The New York *Herald* contained the announcement that Gen. Logan had been telegraphed not to start for Baltimore until he was satisfied that he was wanted. It was evidently the belief of the Radicals that he was ready to move, and feared that he would start too soon.

Now we would ask, what does all this mean, if it is not the preparation for civil war? It is the same system of tactics as that pursued by the Radicals of the North that led to our late war. The leaders in the Radical party show no disposition to change their programme, but grow more bold every day. Certain it is that we are now treading upon civil war, and the above facts show conclusively that the threats of the Radicals are not simply boasts.

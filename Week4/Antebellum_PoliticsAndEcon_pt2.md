### Lecture 7, Part 2: Governors, Andrew Jackson & Indian Removal — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Founding of Memphis & Early Governors
* **Memphis (founded 1819):** Established by Andrew Jackson, General James Winchester, and Judge John Overton; grew slowly at first before booming with the expansion of the cotton economy southward.
* **Willie Blount:** Governor 1809–1815; half-brother of William Blount (the earlier territorial governor).
* **Joseph McMinn:** East Tennessee governor who succeeded Willie Blount before political power shifted toward Middle Tennessee.
* **Sam Houston:** Later a major figure in Texas history; served briefly as Tennessee governor before resigning.
* **William Carroll:** A Nashville businessman and reform governor (elected multiple times in the 1820s–1830s) who improved state treasury practices, reformed criminal sentencing (including the death penalty), and supported new state institutions.

##### Party Politics of the 1820s–1830s
* **Whig Party:** Emerging Tennessee party favoring an active federal government and infrastructure improvements.
* **Democratic Party:** The Jacksonian party with strong presence in the state, closely tied to loyalty toward Andrew Jackson.
* **Pro-Jackson vs. Anti-Jackson Factions:** Despite Jackson being Tennessee's first U.S. president, a notable anti-Jackson political current existed within the state during the 1830s.

##### Andrew Jackson: Rise, Persona & Legacy
* **Andrew Jackson:** Rose from humble frontier origins in the Carolina backcountry to national fame after the Battle of New Orleans (1812); lost the deadlocked 1824 election (decided in the House) to John Quincy Adams, then won the presidency in 1828 and 1832.
* **Rachel Jackson:** Jackson's wife, subjected to vicious personal attacks by his opponents (including bigamy accusations stemming from her earlier divorce) during the 1828 campaign; died in 1828, which Jackson blamed on campaign-related stress.
* **Daniel Walker Howe:** Historian quoted describing Jackson's "profoundly authoritarian instincts" beneath his democratic rhetoric.
* **King Andrew / "Common Man" Populism:** Jackson's dual image — criticized by opponents as an aspiring monarch, while his rough frontier background and blunt, combative personality endeared him to populist supporters.
* **Nullification Crisis & Indian Removal:** Two defining, contrasting episodes of Jackson's presidency — he stood firm against South Carolina's Fire-Eaters in the Nullification Crisis, while aggressively pursuing Indian Removal.

##### The Cherokee Nation & Removal
* **Cherokee Nation (1817 Constitution):** Adopted a Republican-style government; some Cherokees accepted Christianity, held enslaved people, and participated in the slave trade — a nation of internal complexity, also known for taking in free people and harboring escaped enslaved people.
* **Sequoyah:** Cherokee leader who developed an 86-character syllabary for the Cherokee language, enabling the bilingual newspaper the *Cherokee Phoenix*.
* **Indian Removal Act (1830):** Federal legislation that, backed by Georgia and Alabama's legal and political pressure, forced the Cherokee to sign a removal treaty (concluded December 1835) and undertake forced migration (the Trail of Tears) in the late 1830s, killing an estimated 4,000 of roughly 16,000 Cherokees forced from their homeland.
* **John Ross / Chattanooga:** Cherokee leader who operated a trading post and ferry at the site that, after Cherokee removal, white settlers turned into the city of Chattanooga.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Sectional shift in political power:** Governorship and political influence steadily migrated from East Tennessee toward Middle Tennessee across the early 19th century.
2. **Jackson's contradictions:** A man of humble, rough origins who nonetheless enslaved people and orchestrated indigenous dispossession, while cultivating a "defender of the common man" image — his biography is a study in contradiction.
3. **Land pressure driving removal:** Cherokee internal "coalescence" and institution-building (constitution, syllabary, newspaper) could not offset the external pressure of settlers coveting Cherokee land, culminating in the Indian Removal Act and the Trail of Tears.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture moves from Tennessee's early state governors through the rise of Andrew Jackson to the Indian Removal Act's devastating impact on the Cherokee.

1. **Finalizing the 1834 Constitution and Founding Memphis (1819–1835):** The 1834 constitution was formally ratified in the spring of **1835**. Separately, **Memphis** was founded in 1819 by Andrew Jackson, General James Winchester, and Judge John Overton, growing slowly before booming alongside the expanding cotton economy.
2. **Early Governors and Shifting Power (1809–1830s):** Tennessee's early 19th-century governors — **Willie Blount** (1809–1815), **Joseph McMinn**, **Sam Houston** (who resigned abruptly), and reform governor **William Carroll** — trace a gradual shift of political power from East to Middle Tennessee. Carroll, a relatable self-made Nashville businessman, reformed the treasury and criminal sentencing.
3. **Party Politics and Jackson's Rise:** By the late 1820s–1830s, Tennessee politics split between the **Whig Party** and the **Democratic Party**, with allegiance often tracking support for or opposition to **Andrew Jackson**. Jackson rose from humble frontier origins to fame at the Battle of New Orleans, lost the contested 1824 election to **John Quincy Adams**, then won the presidency in 1828 and 1832 — an election cycle marked by vicious personal attacks, including on his wife **Rachel Jackson**.
4. **Jackson as a Complicated Figure:** Historian **Daniel Walker Howe** is quoted describing Jackson's authoritarian instincts beneath his democratic image. Jackson enslaved people, adopted an indigenous boy, stood firm against secessionist "Fire-Eaters" in the **Nullification Crisis**, and yet aggressively pursued **Indian Removal** — cementing a legacy of contradiction.
5. **The Indian Removal Act and Cherokee Removal (1830–1835):** Despite the Cherokee Nation's 1817 constitution, adoption of Christianity by some members, and Sequoyah's syllabary enabling the *Cherokee Phoenix* newspaper, mounting pressure from Georgia and Alabama culminated in the **Indian Removal Act of 1830**. The Cherokee signed a removal treaty in December 1835, and forced migration in the late 1830s killed an estimated 4,000 of the roughly 16,000 Cherokees removed — with **Chattanooga** founded on the site of Cherokee leader John Ross's former trading post.

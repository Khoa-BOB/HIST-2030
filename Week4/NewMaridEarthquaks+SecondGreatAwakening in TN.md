## Summary

### Lecture 6: The New Madrid Earthquakes & the Second Great Awakening in Tennessee — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### The New Madrid Earthquakes (1811–1812)
* **New Madrid Seismic Zone:** An intraplate fault zone (not a plate boundary) centered in southeastern Missouri and extending into southern Illinois, western Kentucky, western Tennessee, and northeastern Arkansas.
* **The "Big Three" Earthquakes (Dec. 1811–Feb. 1812):** A series of major earthquakes estimated at roughly magnitude 7–8 on the Richter scale, plus hundreds of moderate and thousands of smaller aftershocks over several months.
* **Sand Blows & Liquefaction:** Distinctive surface effects in which saturated soil liquefied under seismic stress, shooting liquefied sand upward through volcano-like cones.
* **Reelfoot Lake / Reelfoot Creek:** A shallow flooded forest in western Tennessee formed when the earthquakes caused land to sink and impound Reelfoot Creek; today a major regional fishing and eagle-watching site.
* **The Mississippi River Reversal:** A legendary but historically documented effect in which the force of the earthquakes caused the Mississippi River to appear to run backward briefly.

##### Indigenous Interpretation & Historiography
* **Conevery Bolton Valencius:** Historian quoted describing indigenous and settler interpretations of the earthquakes as "powerful spiritual signs."
* **Tecumseh & the Shawnee Prophet (Tenskwatawa):** Led a political and cultural pan-Indian confederation movement that interpreted the earthquakes as a sign of the Great Spirit's anger at the loss of Indian lands and culture.
* **Cherokee Spiritual Revival:** A wave of prophetic visions and community revival among the Cherokee, beginning in 1811 and intensifying after the earthquakes, urging resistance to Americanization and dislocation.
* **Creek Civil Conflict:** Referenced alongside the Cherokee revival and Tecumseh's confederation as one of three parallel Native political/religious responses to the era's upheaval (a nod to the Red Stick/Creek War).

##### The Second Great Awakening
* **Second Great Awakening:** An early 19th-century religious revival movement emphasizing personal salvation, being "born again," and emotionally intense worship (weeping, shouting, shaking).
* **Camp Meetings & Circuit Riders:** Large outdoor religious gatherings (some drawing up to 25,000 people) organized by traveling Methodist, Baptist, and Presbyterian ministers, which transformed Tennessee's reputation from religiously indifferent to a hotbed of revivalism.
* **Spiritual vs. Scientific Interpretation:** The earthquakes' physical sensations (shaking, sulfur smells, "healing" springs) were interpreted by many contemporaries through the lens of religious revival occurring simultaneously.

##### Later Scientific Rediscovery
* **Center for Earthquake Research and Information (CERI), University of Memphis:** Along with scientists at St. Louis University, revived serious scientific and historical study of the New Madrid earthquakes starting in the 1970s, after decades in which Americans had largely forgotten — or even doubted — their significance.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Shared language of upheaval:** Indigenous communities and white settlers both reached for spiritual explanations of the earthquakes, but indigenous interpretations were filtered through the specific, concurrent pressure of land dispossession and forced assimilation.
2. **Coincidence of physical and spiritual shaking:** The literal shaking of the earth reinforced and amplified the emotionally physical worship style of the Second Great Awakening, which was already spreading through Tennessee and Kentucky at the same time.
3. **Memory and forgetting:** Despite enormous contemporary documentation, the New Madrid earthquakes faded from public memory over the 19th century, only being seriously re-examined by scientists and historians starting in the 1970s.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture examines the 1811–1812 New Madrid earthquakes as both a geological event and a cultural moment, exploring how different communities interpreted the disaster and connecting it to the contemporaneous Second Great Awakening.

1. **The Earthquakes Themselves (Dec. 1811–Feb. 1812):** A series of powerful intraplate earthquakes, estimated at magnitude 7–8, struck the **New Madrid Seismic Zone**, an area spanning southeastern Missouri, southern Illinois, western Kentucky, and western Tennessee. The quakes caused dramatic environmental effects — liquefaction and sand blows, uplifted land forming visible domes, the apparent reversal of the Mississippi River, and the formation of **Reelfoot Lake** in western Tennessee — and were felt as far away as New Hampshire and the East Coast.
2. **Indigenous Interpretation:** Historian **Conevery Bolton Valencius**'s work shows that indigenous communities, already under severe pressure from land cessions and forced assimilation, interpreted the earthquakes as spiritual signs. This helped fuel three parallel movements: **Tecumseh** and the Shawnee Prophet's pan-Indian political-religious confederation, a prophetic revival among the **Cherokee**, and unrest among the Creek.
3. **Settler Interpretation and Scientific Curiosity:** White settlers and traders in the region likewise read the earthquakes in spiritual terms — as portents of damnation or divine anger — while also being among the first Americans to take up amateur scientific study of seismic activity, despite lacking modern technology.
4. **The Second Great Awakening:** The physical experience of the earth shaking dovetailed with the emotionally intense religious revivalism of the **Second Great Awakening**, which was sweeping Tennessee and Kentucky at the same time through Methodist, Baptist, and Presbyterian camp meetings and circuit-riding preachers — transforming a region once seen as religiously indifferent into one of significant revival activity.
5. **Forgetting and Rediscovery:** Despite extensive contemporary documentation, the earthquakes' significance faded from American memory over the 19th century — some 20th-century scientists even doubted they had occurred — until researchers at St. Louis University and the University of Memphis's **Center for Earthquake Research and Information (CERI)** revived serious study of the region starting in the 1970s, combining geological and historical evidence.

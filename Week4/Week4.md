# Week 4 Summary & Study Focus

**Big picture:** Week 4 covers roughly 1811–1849, opening with the New Madrid earthquakes and the Second Great Awakening's religious upheaval, then moving into antebellum Tennessee politics — the 1834 constitutional convention, the rise (and repeated local defeats) of Andrew Jackson protégé James K. Polk, and the Indian Removal Act's forced removal of the Cherokee — before closing out the decade with the Whig–Democrat rivalry and the origins of Tennessee's "Volunteer State" nickname.

### Quick recap
- **Lecture 6 – New Madrid Earthquakes & Second Great Awakening:** The 1811–1812 New Madrid earthquakes devastate the Mississippi River region (forming Reelfoot Lake, briefly reversing the river), and are interpreted through both indigenous spiritual movements (Tecumseh's confederation, a Cherokee prophetic revival) and the contemporaneous **Second Great Awakening**, whose emotional camp-meeting revivalism was sweeping Tennessee and Kentucky at the same time.
- **Lecture 7, pt. 1 – Antebellum Politics & Economy:** The 1834 Tennessee constitutional convention overhauls taxation (moving to value-based taxation of property and enslaved people) and drops property requirements for voting and office — while simultaneously inserting the word "white" into the franchise clause, disfranchising free people of color and rejecting some 30 gradual-emancipation petitions.
- **Lecture 7, pt. 2 – Antebellum Politics & Economy (cont.):** Traces Tennessee's early governors (Willie Blount → Joseph McMinn → Sam Houston → William Carroll) and the founding of Memphis (1819), then pivots to Andrew Jackson's rise from frontier origins through the contested 1824 election, the vicious 1828 campaign, and his complicated legacy — standing firm in the Nullification Crisis while aggressively pursuing the **Indian Removal Act of 1830**, which forced the Cherokee's removal by 1835–1838.
- **Lecture 7, pt. 3 – More Antebellum Politics:** Follows the Whig–Democrat rivalry through the 1840s: **James K. Polk** wins the Tennessee governorship in 1839, loses it twice to Whig "Lean Jimmy" Jones, then wins the presidency in 1844 (losing his own home state to Henry Clay in the process). The lecture also traces Tennessee's "Volunteer State" nickname to the Mexican-American War (and, further back, the War of 1812), and closes with the decade's remaining governors' races (A.V. Brown, Neill S. Brown, Trousdale).

### What to focus on
1. **Two very different lenses on the same era:** The New Madrid earthquakes lecture is about religion, disaster, and meaning-making, while the two antebellum politics lectures are about formal political institutions — but both unfold in the same early-19th-century Tennessee and both intersect with the era's treatment of indigenous peoples and enslaved people.
2. **The 1834 constitution's double edge:** Don't treat it as simple democratization — it expanded suffrage and officeholding for white men while simultaneously disfranchising free people of color and constitutionally barring future emancipation legislation. This is the single most important nuance of the week.
3. **Indian Removal as a recurring throughline:** The Cherokee's institution-building (1817 constitution, Sequoyah's syllabary, the *Cherokee Phoenix*) did not protect them from the Indian Removal Act of 1830 — land pressure from Georgia and Alabama, backed by Jackson, culminated in the 1835 removal treaty and the Trail of Tears, founding Chattanooga on the site of Cherokee leader John Ross's trading post.
4. **Jackson's contradictions:** Humble frontier origins and a populist "common man" image, alongside enslaving people, orchestrating indigenous dispossession, and (per historian Daniel Walker Howe) "profoundly authoritarian instincts" beneath his democratic rhetoric.
5. **Personality vs. party in 1840s elections:** Polk's repeated losses to the charismatic Whig "Lean Jimmy" Jones — and his own loss of Tennessee to Henry Clay in 1844 even while winning the presidency nationally — show that charisma and local dynamics could override party lines and "favorite son" status.
6. **Anchor dates:** 1811–1812 (New Madrid earthquakes), 1817 (Cherokee constitution), 1819 (Memphis founded), 1824 (Jackson loses to J.Q. Adams), 1828/1832 (Jackson wins presidency), 1830 (Indian Removal Act), May 1834–1835 (TN constitutional convention and ratification), 1835 (Cherokee removal treaty), 1839 (Polk wins TN governorship), 1841/1843 (Polk loses to Jones), 1844 (Polk wins presidency), 1846–1848 (Mexican-American War), 1849 (Trousdale wins governorship).

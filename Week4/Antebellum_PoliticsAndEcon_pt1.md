## Summary

### Lecture 7, Part 1: Antebellum Politics & the 1834 Constitution — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Constitutional Documents & Provisions
* **1796 Tennessee Constitution:** The state's founding charter; notably allowed property-holding free men, including free people of color, to vote, and taxed land and enslaved people uniformly regardless of value.
* **1834 Tennessee Constitution:** Drafted by a convention that met in Nashville starting May 1834 and ratified in 1835; overhauled taxation, reapportioned the legislature, dropped property requirements for voting and office-holding, and inserted the word "white" into the franchise clause.
* **Ad Valorem Taxation:** The 1834 constitution's new principle that property (including enslaved people) should be taxed according to its value, reversing the 1796 constitution's uniform-taxation approach.

##### Political Context & Movements
* **Age of Jackson / Jacksonian Democracy:** The broader national push, associated with Andrew Jackson's presidency, toward more democratic government — expanding suffrage and elected office to more white men by removing property qualifications.
* **East Tennessee Anti-Slavery Movement:** A gradual-emancipation movement active in early 19th-century East Tennessee that had begun losing momentum by the 1830s but still generated roughly 30 petitions to the 1834 convention.
* **West Tennessee Plantation Society:** The rapidly growing, cotton-and-slavery-based section of the state whose expansion sharpened the contrast with East Tennessee and shaped the convention's debates over slavery and taxation.

##### Key Debates at the 1834 Convention
* **Suffrage Restriction / Disfranchisement:** Delegates debated — and ultimately approved — stripping free people of color of the voting rights they had held under the 1796 constitution, even while expanding suffrage for white men.
* **Gradual Emancipation Petitions:** About 30 petitions (mostly from East Tennessee, none from West Tennessee) proposed gradual emancipation schemes (e.g., freeing children born after a set date); a three-person committee rejected them, framing slavery as a "necessary evil, not a positive good."
* **Emancipation Advocates' Response:** Petitioners charged that the committee's rejection was "subversive of Republican principles" and biblical teaching, and that it amounted to an apology for slavery.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Democratization for whom:** The 1834 convention expanded political participation for white men while simultaneously narrowing it for free people of color — a "double-edged" reform that can't be understood as democratization in the abstract.
2. **Taxation as a proxy for slavery's growing economic weight:** Shifting from uniform to value-based taxation of enslaved people reflected West Tennessee's growing reliance on enslaved labor and its rising political influence.
3. **Sectional divide within the state:** East Tennessee's fading anti-slavery movement and West Tennessee's expanding plantation economy pulled convention debates in opposite directions on slavery and taxation.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture examines the 1834 Tennessee constitutional convention as the central political event of the antebellum era, tracing how it both expanded and restricted democracy along racial lines.

1. **Background and Context (1796–1830s):** The 1796 constitution had granted relatively broad voting rights, including to propertied free people of color. By the 1830s, the nationwide **Age of Jackson** was pushing states to remove property requirements for voting and office-holding, while Tennessee's growing sectional divide — a fading **anti-slavery movement in East Tennessee** versus an expanding **plantation and slave society in West Tennessee** — set the stage for a fraught debate over slavery and taxation.
2. **The 1834 Convention's Reforms (May 1834):** Delegates gathered in Nashville and overhauled the tax system, shifting from uniform taxation of property and enslaved people to **ad valorem (value-based) taxation**. They restructured the state Supreme Court and reapportioned the legislature, and — most significantly for suffrage — removed property requirements for voting and for holding legislative or gubernatorial office.
3. **Disfranchisement of Free People of Color:** Even as delegates expanded the franchise for white men, they voted to insert the word "white" into the constitution's franchise clause, stripping free people of color of voting rights they had held since 1796 — a deliberate reversal debated over several weeks.
4. **The Emancipation Petitions:** Around 30 petitions calling for gradual emancipation, overwhelmingly from East Tennessee, were referred to a three-person committee, which rejected them, declaring slavery "a necessary evil, not a positive good." Emancipation advocates protested that this rejection betrayed Republican and biblical principles; the convention subsequently barred the legislature from ever acting on emancipation, by a close 31–27 vote.
5. **Outcome:** The 1834 constitution (ratified in 1835) left a mixed legacy: expanded suffrage and officeholding for white Tennesseans, alongside the disfranchisement of free Black Tennesseans and a hardened constitutional bar on future emancipation.

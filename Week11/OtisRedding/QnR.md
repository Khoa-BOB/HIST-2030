# Question:
In the assigned episode, the contours of Otis Redding's life and his death are outlined. Summarize what you learned from the assigned episode, and what do you think made Otis Redding special? Put simply, of all of the spectacular musicians at Stax Records in Memphis, why did Otis Redding stand out? Be specific. Use examples from the episode and from at least one of Otis Redding's songs.

# Response:
Otis Redding grew up poor in the segregated South, one of five kids with a father sick with TB, in a housing project called Bellevue. He made his records at Stax, an old cinema on McLemore Avenue in Memphis with white owners and a mix of Black and white musicians. With Booker T. and the M.G.'s behind him, he recorded *Otis Blue* in 24 hours, then toured in crammed cars, playing a Black club one night and an all-white frat party the next. A 1966 European tour, where the Beatles sent Bentley limos to meet the band at Heathrow, and the Monterey festival in 1967 brought him much larger audiences. To keep up with the gigs he bought a second-hand Beechcraft 18, and in December 1967 it crashed into Lake Monona on approach to Madison, Wisconsin. He was 26. The official cause stayed "undetermined" for almost 50 years, until Jonathan Gould's book pointed to a pilot with under 100 hours in twin-engine planes and ice on the Beech's thick wings. "Dock of the Bay" came out after the funeral, went to number one on the pop charts, and sold more than a million copies.

I think Otis stood out because listeners believed him. The episode makes clear that the people around him were spectacular: Al Jackson was the best drummer in town, Booker T. had "Green Onions," and Steve Cropper's guitar arpeggios shaped the Stax sound. But Otis was the center of how those songs got made. In the studio he sang the horn lines to the horn players, worked out chords with Cropper, and sang shirtless and sweating for hours. The episode contrasts this with Motown, which taught its stars to be smooth and dress smart, and with James Brown, who sang about sex like a preacher. Otis took that same mix of the holy and the dirty and made something purer. The narrator's word for it is empathy: "His pain, your pain," and yet he makes you feel better.

Monterey shows what that could do. Otis played after midnight, in cold rain, in a lime green or bright blue Lansky Brothers suit, in front of an all-white rock crowd, with no other Black performers on the bill that day. He yelled, "This is the love crowd, right?" and won them over. He closed with "I've Been Loving You Too Long," a song the episode describes through its arpeggios, its groove, and Otis pleading and sighing over them. Nothing about that performance relied on polish.

"Dock of the Bay" is the best example of how far he might have gone. After hearing *Sgt. Pepper*, he studied how the Beatles layered and stripped back their songs and told bigger stories in their lyrics. The result is a quiet song about a tired man watching the tide roll away, and when Otis forgot some of the words, he whistled instead. He knew what he had, telling a friend, "This is my first million seller." Cropper said that when Otis walked into a room, "the whole place lit up," and Jim Stewart said that after his death, "Something was taken out and never replaced." At Stax, plenty of people could play. Otis was the one they could not replace.

# High Mercury Levels Found At Civic Center

**By CAROLYN SHOULDERS**
*The Tennessean*, Metro/State, Section B, p. 11 — Monday, January 23, 1984

---

Mercury levels up to 23 times the state guidelines have been found in soil around the Oak Ridge Civic Center and jogging track, according to the U.S. Department of Energy.

The mercury contamination, recorded in soil samples taken last month, came from mud in the banks of the East Fork Poplar Creek, which was used to fill in a city sewer line in 1982, said Wayne Hibbitts, acting chief of the environmental protection branch.

**TOXIC AND** radioactive wastes from DOE's Y-12 nuclear weapons plant have contaminated the creek for decades, but the contamination was kept secret until a 5-year-old mercury contamination report was declassified last May.

"Had we known about the contamination earlier, we would have avoided hauling dredged creek soil," said Jim Harless, environmental coordinator for Oak Ridge.

A radiological survey also shows the Civic Center area has a higher radiation level than normal background levels because uranium and thorium are present in the creek sediment used over the sewer line, Hibbitts said.

**BOTH** Hibbitts and an official with the state Department of Public Health said the radiation levels, while higher than background levels, do not exceed state environmental guidelines on radiation.

The soil samples, taken as part of an extensive study that resulted from the news of the mercury contamination from the Y-12 plant, show mercury readings of up to 280 parts per million. That is 23 times more than the 12 ppm guideline used by the state to determine whether to take further action.

The highest mercury reading is 59 times the normal range of mercury in soils, listed as 0.01 to 4.7 ppm, according to the National Academy of Sciences.

**HOWEVER,** 42 of 68 samples taken in the Civic Center area of downtown Oak Ridge showed readings below the normal range as determined by the National Academy of Sciences.

The mercury, which is highly toxic if ingested by humans, does not pose an immediate public health threat because there does not appear to be a likely way of getting soil contamination into the human body, said Hibbitts.

A city study group, headed by Harless, will determine whether the cleanup or removal of contaminated soil at the Civic Center is needed immediately.

The Oak Ridge Associated Universities, which is conducting the soil tests, has not completed its sampling of the city sewer line where contamination is suspected, Hibbitts said.

# Oak Ridge Lab's Wastes Reported Affecting Rivers

**TENNESSEAN Wire Reports**
*The Tennessean*, Metro/State, Section D, p. 50 — Sunday, November 6, 1983

---

Hazardous waste — including deadly plutonium — has flowed from an Oak Ridge National Laboratory lake into the Tennessee and Clinch rivers, contaminating fish with radiation, according to a state report.

The report by the state Public Health and Environment Department cited the U.S. Energy Department for a wide array of violations linked to the dumping of wastes at the Oak Ridge laboratory.

**MEANWHILE,** 6th District Rep. Albert Gore Jr. has said that Energy Department officials provided "misleading information" about mercury contamination and other pollution problems at the department's Oak Ridge nuclear complex.

Gore, a Carthage Democrat, voiced the charge Friday in releasing results of a congressional investigation into environmental problems at the complex, which includes Oak Ridge National Laboratory, a uranium-enrichment facility and the Energy Department's Y-12 nuclear weapons plant.

The congressional report concluded that although "discharged and spilled mercury is present in significant concentrations...in the Oak Ridge area, mercury contamination does not present an immediate danger to the public health."

**HOWEVER, IT** added that "the potential for long-term harm from mercury is harder to resolve."

Issued by the staff of two House Science and Technology subcommittees, the report urged the department to implement a "comprehensive plan to gather additional data" on pollution in the Oak Ridge area.

A multi-agency task force is looking at a $1.5 million sampling program to test for "mercury and a number of other potential pollutants coming from the operation here," according to Wayne Range, an Energy Department spokesman at the Oak Ridge complex.

**THE TASK FORCE,** he said, was established following the initial reports last spring about mercury contamination. The panel includes representatives from the department itself, the Environmental Protection Agency, the Tennessee Valley Authority, the U.S. Geological Survey, the state of Tennessee and the city of Oak Ridge.

"We have taken a number of steps already in cooperation with EPA and the state of Tennessee to assess the conditions and move toward mitigating those determined to need work," Range said.

About 2.4 million pounds of mercury were discharged from the Oak Ridge complex over an 11-year period during the 1950s and 1960s, according to the report.

**GORE CHARGED** that the Energy Department "did not properly notify area residents and officials about environmental dangers.

"Instead," he said, "DOE released sometimes misleading information about the mercury and failed to fully cooperate in efforts to address the environmental concerns."

He called waste management practices at the Oak Ridge complex "inexcusably outdated," despite availability of personnel capable of planning and carrying out state-of-the-art pollution control methods.

**THE REPORT,** prepared by a Science and Technology subcommittee that Gore chairs, and another subcommittee on energy, research and production chaired by 3rd District Rep. Marilyn Lloyd also recommended establishing "an independent scientific review panel ... to collect data on pollutants and to help restore DOE's credibility with the local community and officials," according to Gore, but Range said efforts already are underway to select such a panel.

According to the state's report, pollution has turned White Oak Lake into one of the most radioactive lakes in the nation and other leaks at the laboratory have contaminated underground water supplies that could lead to private drinking wells.

"In its present condition, White Oak Lake presents an ongoing and primary source of radioactive contamination of fish, water and sediments of the Clinch and Tennessee Rivers," the report said.

**THE WASTES** are discharged into White Oak Creek, which winds through the laboratory grounds before flowing into the lake. Radioactive elements found in the lake's sediments included plutonium, cobalt, cesium, tritium, zirconium, iodine and others.

The report recommended cleaning up the lake and closing it to the discharges.

"Based on the information available, cleanup and closure would seem an appropriate action," the report said. "Plans and implementation schedules must be established to eliminate this source of contamination rather than allow releases indefinitely."

Wayne Hibbitts, chief of the Energy Department's environmental protection branch, said he was surprised by the recommendation and said the state needs to examine ways to prevent the pollution from reaching the rivers.

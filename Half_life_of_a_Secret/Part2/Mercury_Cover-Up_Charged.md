# Mercury Cover-Up Charged

**United Press International**
*The Tennessean*, Front Page (p. 1) — Saturday, May 21, 1983

---

OAK RIDGE (UPI) — A research biologist who investigated mercury contamination at the Energy Department's nuclear weapons plant here has charged DOE officials tried to "cover up" information about the mercury pollution.

Dr. Steve Gough, who formerly worked at DOE's Oak Ridge National Laboratory, said that for several months the department suppressed test results showing high concentrations of mercury in plant life at East Fork Poplar Creek.

**GOUGH SAID** DOE showed his findings to state health officials only after he blew the whistle by telling an independent Energy Department investigator about the alleged cover-up. The state Health Department subsequently posted signs along the creek warning fishermen not to eat fish caught there.

In December 1981, Gough collected plant samples from the creek and had the material tested for mercury contamination by his brother Larry, a geochemist with the U.S. Geological Survey.

Laboratory analysis revealed the plants contained the largest concentrations of mercury ever found in plant material analyzed by the Geological Survey.

**GOUGH SAID** he told his superiors at the national laboratory about his findings in February 1982, but they did nothing.

"Cover-up would be the best term for it," Gough said. "They weren't willing to aggressively tackle the problem themselves.

"A problem of that magnitude should have been handled straightforward with the state and other regulatory agencies."

**DOE DISCLOSED** Tuesday it has released "to the environment" an estimated 2.4 million pounds of mercury at the Y-12 nuclear weapons plant site since 1950. Most of the toxic substance was released between 1955 and 1958.

East Fork Poplar Creek flows through Y-12 and winds through the city of Oak Ridge.

Energy Department spokesman Wayne Range said about 475,000 pounds of the mercury released from Y-12 wound up in the creek. Most of the mercury — 1.9 million pounds — was spilled and seeped into the ground, where it is now trapped within geologic formations.

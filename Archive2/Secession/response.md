# Secession and Tennessee’s Changing Political Debate

## Introduction

I searched ProQuest Historical Newspapers for “secession” to examine how Tennesseans discussed disunion before the Civil War. The two articles I found, both published in Nashville’s *Republican Banner*, reveal a change from celebrating Tennessee’s earlier loyalty to the Union in 1851 to confronting an immediate secession movement in 1860. Both show that Tennessee communities actively debated their relationship with the federal government.

The first article, “Position of Tennessee in 1832–3,” appeared on June 5, 1851, page 2. As sectional tensions increased in 1851, the newspaper revisited Union meetings held in Henry and Hawkins Counties during the Nullification Crisis of 1832. Citizens gathered at courthouses, listened to the Declaration of Independence and President Andrew Jackson’s proclamation against nullification, and adopted resolutions promising to preserve the Union—even by force if peaceful measures failed. The lists of organizers and instructions to send the resolutions to political leaders and newspapers demonstrate how public meetings, print networks, and county institutions connected local communities to national politics.

The second article, “Secession Meeting in Winchester, Tennessee,” appeared on December 1, 1860, page 2, shortly after Abraham Lincoln’s election. It reports that P. Turney and M. Turney demanded immediate, separate-state secession before Lincoln’s inauguration. They praised South Carolina and argued that Northern resistance to the Fugitive Slave Law had already dissolved the Union. Col. A. S. Colyar opposed immediate secession, arguing that South Carolina should consult Tennessee and other border states and that a state convention should decide the issue. The correspondent was encouraged that many Democrats would abandon party loyalty to support the Union.

I did not find a unified Tennessee position or much discussion of everyday economic life in these articles. Instead, they reveal disagreement over political procedure, federal authority, slavery, and whether Tennessee should follow South Carolina. Compared with Archive 1, which emphasized the Atlantic slave trade and relied heavily on national and international reprints, these articles focus more directly on Tennessee’s county meetings, political leaders, and local responses to national events.

## Conclusion

Together, the articles capture a major transition in Tennessee politics. The 1851 article used memories of 1832 to warn against disunion, while the 1860 report shows secession becoming an urgent and divisive possibility. Tennessee’s newspapers therefore provide evidence of politically organized communities struggling over Union, slavery, state sovereignty, and the proper method of collective decision-making.

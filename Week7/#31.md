## Summary

### Lecture 31: The End of the American Civil War in Tennessee — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Wartime Government
* **Union Occupation of Tennessee:** By the war's middle years Tennessee was effectively under **Union control**, though Confederate **guerrilla bands** kept up sabotage. The state's Confederate government lost power.
* **Andrew Johnson, Military Governor (1862–1865):** Appointed by **Lincoln**. A Unionist who saw secessionists as traitors, he was hated by many in Middle and West Tennessee, who saw *him* as the traitor. He tried conciliation at first, then turned to **harsh measures**: arrests, imprisonment, and **shutting down secessionist newspapers**. He served until becoming **vice president** in **1865**.

##### Emancipation in Tennessee
* **Self-Emancipation:** The disruption of war gave enslaved people the chance to free themselves. It was never easy or without risk, and some were forced back into slavery.
* **Emancipation Proclamation (January 1863):** Issued after the preliminary proclamation of **late 1862**. **Tennessee was exempted**, but Union occupation brought a **de facto end of slavery**. As one Northern official said, "slavery was dead in Tennessee."
* **Changing Meaning of the War:** Lincoln began the war to **save the Union**. By late **1862–1863** he framed it as a **war of emancipation**.
* **Contraband Camps (from August 1862):** Ordered by a Union general for self-freed refugees at places including **Clarksville**, **Henderson**, and **Pulaski**. They offered shelter, rations, work, medicine, and clothing, but were crowded and prone to disease. Black laborers in places like **Nashville** worked for the Union army.
* **Scale of Freedom:** About **276,000** enslaved Tennesseans became free. About **20,000** Black Tennesseans fought for the Union army, a powerful symbol of formerly enslaved men fighting those who had held them in bondage.

##### Final Battles
* **Fort Pillow Massacre (April 1864):** Fort Pillow sat on the Mississippi about **40 miles north of Memphis**. A white garrison there had been reinforced by about **262 Black soldiers** from **Fort Pickering**. **Nathan Bedford Forrest's** cavalry took it "by storm." Under the Confederacy's **no-quarter policy** toward Black soldiers, and driven by hatred, Confederates inflicted **238 casualties** on the 262 Black soldiers. Confederates claimed the Black troops refused to surrender, but **survivors' testimony** told a very different story.
* **Battle of Franklin (November 1864):** Covered in the video (Lecture 28).
* **Battle of Nashville (December 1864):** The **last major battle** in Tennessee. The Union army smashed **John Bell Hood's** army, which retreated to Mississippi. The war in Tennessee was essentially over by the **end of 1864**, and the war itself ended in **spring 1865**.

##### Women's Wartime Experience
* **Women in the War:** The PBS clips (Lecture 30) cover how enslaved and freed women, Unionist women, and Confederate women experienced the war. Their wartime roles would shape their roles in **Reconstruction** and the **New South**, and this material will be on the **final exam**.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Occupation brought federal rule:** Union control put Andrew Johnson in charge of a state where many saw him as a traitor, and his early conciliation hardened into repression.
2. **Freedom came before the law:** Although the Emancipation Proclamation exempted Tennessee, self-emancipation under Union occupation ended slavery there in practice.
3. **Black soldiers changed the war and provoked fury:** About 20,000 Black Tennesseans in Union blue made the war's meaning unmistakable and drew extreme Confederate violence, as at Fort Pillow.
4. **The final collapse:** Franklin and Nashville destroyed Hood's army and ended major fighting in Tennessee by the close of 1864.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture wraps up the Civil War in Tennessee, covering Union occupation, Andrew Johnson's military governorship, emancipation, Fort Pillow, and the final battles.

1. **Occupation & Andrew Johnson (1862–1865):** As the Union army occupied the state, Tennessee's Confederate government lost power, and **Lincoln** appointed **Andrew Johnson** military governor. A Unionist who saw secessionists as traitors, Johnson was hated by many in Middle and West Tennessee. After failed attempts at conciliation, he used **arrests**, imprisonment, and the closing of secessionist newspapers to crush resistance. He served until becoming **vice president** in **1865**.
2. **Emancipation in Tennessee (1862–1863):** The disruption of war let enslaved people **free themselves**, though not without risk. The **Emancipation Proclamation (January 1863)** **exempted Tennessee**, yet with Union troops present, "slavery was dead in Tennessee" in practice. Lincoln increasingly framed the conflict as a **war of emancipation**. From **August 1862**, **contraband camps** at sites like **Clarksville** and **Pulaski** offered shelter and work despite crowding and disease.
3. **Freedom and Black Soldiers:** About **276,000** enslaved Tennesseans became free, and about **20,000** Black Tennesseans fought for the Union, often against the very people who had enslaved them. The lecturer points students to the PBS clips (Lectures 29–30) on the experiences of enslaved, freed, Unionist, and Confederate women, which will matter for the **final exam**.
4. **The Fort Pillow Massacre (April 1864):** At **Fort Pillow**, 40 miles north of Memphis, **Forrest's** cavalry stormed a garrison that included about **262 Black soldiers**. Under the Confederacy's **no-quarter policy**, they suffered **238 casualties**. Confederates claimed the Black troops refused to surrender, but **survivors' accounts** described a massacre, an example of how hate-filled the war's late stages became.
5. **The End in Tennessee (November–December 1864):** After **Franklin**, the **Battle of Nashville** in **December 1864**, the last major battle in the state, shattered **John Bell Hood's** army, which retreated to Mississippi. Sporadic fighting continued, but the war in Tennessee was essentially over by the end of 1864, months before the war ended in **spring 1865**.

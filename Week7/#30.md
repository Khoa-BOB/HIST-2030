### Lecture 30: Women on the Home Front, Refugees, Subsistence Politics & Emancipation (Civil War Documentary) — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Women on the Home Front
* **Frustrated Patriotism:** Elite young Confederate women wrote of wanting to "fight and even die for my country" but being denied "because I am a woman."
* **Ladies' Aid Societies:** Raised money and volunteers and supplied **clothing, blankets, food, and nursing**. The **Society of Southern Mothers**, for example, thanked the ladies of **Memphis** for making garments for sick and wounded soldiers.
* **The Confederacy's Manpower Problem:** About **9 million** people compared with the Union's **22 million**, and **4 million** of them enslaved. That left about **6 million** available, **half of them women**. Waging war forced the government to mobilize people who could not vote, women included.
* **Women Enter Public Work:** Women moved into **teaching**, **factory work** (for example, **250 women and girls** making cartridges in a Confederate armory, with soldiers' families hired first), and **hospital work**. Elite women served as **matrons**, while poor and working-class women became paid nurses and assistants in what had been male professions.

##### Class Conflict & Refugees
* **"A Rich Man's War and a Poor Man's Fight":** Resentment grew over the **Twenty Negro Law**, which exempted one white man per plantation of **20 or more slaves**, and over paid exemptions for the wealthy. Some **75–85%** of military-age white men served.
* **A Violated Contract:** The war was sold to **yeoman** and poor white men as a defense of home and family, yet it took them from home and left wives without labor, while planters' wives kept enslaved workers.
* **Yeoman Women & Petitions:** Women ran farms in a countryside stripped of men and sent **collective petitions to governors** asking for one man to be sent home. They became **arbiters of exemption**, and politicians began treating them as **constituents**.
* **Foraging Armies & Guerrillas:** Tennessee was **second only to Virginia** in number of battlefields. Foraging armies stripped homesteads, and the worst violence against civilians came from **guerrillas and bandits**, which drove many families to become **refugees**.
* **Elizabeth Avery Meriwether:** Banished from **Memphis** by **General William T. Sherman** for opposing the Union occupation while **seven months pregnant** with two small children.

##### Subsistence Politics
* **Food Riots (1863):** Riots broke out across the Confederacy. The largest was the **Richmond Bread Riot**, organized by **Mary Jackson**, a farm wife and soldier's mother who led about **300 women**. In **Salisbury, North Carolina**, women calling themselves "**the Regulators**" demanded "bread or blood."
* **A Deluge of Petitions:** Women barely petitioned governors in 1860–1861, but by **1863** their letters were a flood, evidence of a new **women's political culture** in rural counties.
* **Confederate Welfare System:** In response, Confederate states built relief programs **unparalleled in the Union**. **Georgia** spent more in one year than Massachusetts spent in the whole war. Aid went **first to soldiers' wives**, who became the prototype welfare recipient. Relief eased the crisis for a while but proved **woefully inadequate** as the army took priority.

##### Emancipation & Freedom
* **Escape to Union Lines:** Enslaved men usually fled first. Women and children followed as chances arose, but Union lines did not guarantee **sanctuary**: commanders often turned them away, and thousands of Black women wandered along the **Mississippi River**.
* **Contraband Camps:** Union camps for self-emancipated people in **Middle and West Tennessee**. They were crowded, dirty, sickly tent camps. After the **Emancipation Proclamation** men could be drafted from them, which left the question of what to do with women and children.
* **Freedom's Meanings:** Freed people sought **legal marriage** (mass weddings of 20–30 couples in the camps), **reunited families** separated by sale, and worked to make a living, acquire **land**, get **education**, and become **citizens**.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Total war made women political:** The Confederacy's thin manpower pulled women into public work and politics. Through petitions and riots, poor white women forced their governments to respond.
2. **Class fractures undermined the cause:** Exemptions for slaveholders and the wealthy, along with starvation at home, made yeoman families feel their "contract" with the Confederacy had been broken.
3. **Welfare born of protest:** Confederate relief systems, built around the soldier's wife, came directly out of women's mobilization and food riots.
4. **Freedom as family and citizenship:** For freed people, emancipation meant legal marriage, reunited families, land, education, and citizenship, all pursued from the hard conditions of the contraband camps.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This documentary segment (chapters 4–7) examines how the Civil War transformed the lives of Confederate women, poor white families, and enslaved people in Tennessee and across the South.

1. **Women on the Home Front:** Elite women often resented being kept from serving, so they channeled their patriotism into **Ladies' Aid Societies**. Because the Confederacy had only about **6 million** free people to draw on, half of them women, it had to mobilize women. They entered **teaching**, **cartridge factories**, and **hospitals**, and poor women took paid nursing work in what had been a male profession.
2. **A Rich Man's War and a Poor Man's Fight:** The **Twenty Negro Law** and paid exemptions bred class resentment while **75–85%** of military-age white men served. Yeoman men had been told they were defending their homes, but the war took them away from those homes, leaving wives without help while planters' wives kept enslaved labor.
3. **Struggles and Refugees:** Yeoman women ran farms alone and **petitioned governors** for men to be sent home, becoming arbiters of exemption and political constituents for the first time. Tennessee, **second only to Virginia** in battlefields, suffered foraging armies and especially **guerrillas and bandits**, which created waves of **refugees**, including **Elizabeth Avery Meriwether**, banished from **Memphis** by **Sherman** while pregnant.
4. **Subsistence Politics (1863):** Facing starvation, women rioted. **Mary Jackson** led about **300 women** in the **Richmond Bread Riot**, and North Carolina's "Regulators" demanded "bread or blood." A flood of petitions pushed Confederate states to build **welfare systems** larger than anything in the Union, with **soldiers' wives** served first. Relief eventually proved inadequate as the army consumed the South's resources.
5. **Emancipation and Freedom:** Enslaved men, then women and children, fled to Union lines but found no guaranteed refuge. Many ended up in crowded, disease-ridden **contraband camps** in Middle and West Tennessee. After the war, freed people embraced **legal marriage**, searched for family members sold away, and pursued land, education, and **citizenship** they believed they had earned.

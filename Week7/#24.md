### Lecture 24: American Civil War, Part 1 — Divided Tennessee — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Confederate Tennessee Mobilizes
* **Governor Isham Harris:** Secessionist governor who led Tennessee into the Confederacy. He struggled to supply the state's army and to control Unionist resistance.
* **Joining the Confederacy (July 1861):** After seceding following the war's start in **April 1861**, Tennessee officially became part of the **Confederate States of America** in **July 1861**.
* **Provisional Army of Tennessee Act:** Authorized a state army of **55,000 men** and committed state money to it. **Gideon Pillow**, a Middle Tennessean and **Mexican-American War** veteran, was named commander. Harris also let the Confederate army build **river fortifications around Memphis**.
* **Planter Reluctance:** Recruiting men was easy, but supplying them was hard. Planters, who had the most to gain from a Confederate victory, often refused to lend their **enslaved laborers** for military work or to fight themselves. Enslaved people, meanwhile, would free themselves and offer their labor to the **Union**.

##### East Tennessee Unionism
* **Catherine Rebecca Rutledge King Letter:** An archival letter to **Oliver Caswell King** describing "a great deal of excitement… about the union." It shows Tennesseans sorting themselves, even socially, into Union and anti-Union camps.
* **Dueling Rallies on Gay Street (April 1861):** Pro-Union and secessionist rallies held at the same time in **Knoxville** before secession, a sign of how openly divided East Tennessee was.
* **East Tennessee Unionist Conventions (June 1861):** Unionists met again after the legislature's "declaration of independence," **denounced secession**, and **petitioned for separate statehood** for East Tennessee. The petition was refused.
* **Bridge Burners (November 1861):** East Tennessee Unionists burned railroad bridges. The crackdown that followed brought **five hangings** (two bodies were left hanging for days near **Greeneville**), **martial law**, mass arrests, imprisonment, and **confiscation of property**.
* **Tennessee's Divided Soldiery:** Roughly **30,000–42,000+** white Tennesseans fought for the **Union**, many after fleeing to **Kentucky**, compared with about **120,000** for the **Confederacy**. Others formed violent anti-Confederate **guerrilla bands**.

##### Key Figures
* **Daniel Ellis:** A **Carter County** Unionist and bridge burner who escaped the Confederate dragnet by hiding in the mountains. He became the "great Union guide," leading **thousands** (Union recruits, draft evaders, escaped POWs, and escaped slaves) to Union-held **Kentucky**. He supported his family through guide fees, carrying mail, and **stealing Confederate horses**, became a Union cavalry **captain (January 1865)**, and published *Thrilling Adventures of Daniel Ellis* in **1867**.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Secession did not unify the state:** East Tennessee's Unionists organized immediately, through rallies, conventions, a statehood petition, and sabotage, and were a constant headache for Harris and the Confederacy.
2. **From conciliation to repression:** Harris first tried a soft approach, but the November 1861 bridge burnings brought hangings, martial law, and confiscation, which pushed many Unionists into Union service or guerrilla war.
3. **Those with the most at stake gave the least:** Planters held back enslaved labor from the Confederate war effort, while enslaved people steadily freed themselves and served the Union.
4. **Small-scale resistance added up:** Figures like Daniel Ellis show how local knowledge and conviction let individuals cause cumulative trouble for the Confederacy.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture looks at how divided Tennessee was as it seceded and entered the Civil War, and the problems those divisions caused for Governor Harris and the Confederacy.

1. **A State Taking Sides (Spring 1861):** A letter from **Catherine Rebecca Rutledge King** shows Tennesseans sorting themselves, even socially, into Union and anti-Union camps. In **Knoxville**, Unionists and secessionists held **dueling rallies on Gay Street** in **April 1861**. After the war began, Confederate authorities worried about the many Tennesseans, especially in **East Tennessee**, who would not willingly fight for or support the Confederacy.
2. **Confederate Mobilization (1861):** Tennessee officially joined the **Confederate States of America** in **July 1861**. **Governor Isham Harris** allowed river fortifications around **Memphis**, and the legislature's **Provisional Army of Tennessee Act** authorized **55,000** troops under **Gideon Pillow**. Recruiting was easy but supplying the army was not, and planters often refused to provide **enslaved labor**, even though they had the most to gain from Confederate victory.
3. **East Tennessee Resists (1861):** Unionists met again in **June 1861**, denounced secession, and petitioned for **separate statehood**, which was denied. Harris tried conciliation at first, but in **November 1861** Unionists **burned railroad bridges**. The crackdown included **five hangings** (two bodies left near **Greeneville**), **martial law**, mass arrests, and property confiscation. Many Unionists fled to **Kentucky** to join the Union army, roughly **30,000–42,000+** Tennesseans in all compared with about **120,000** Confederates, and others formed **guerrilla bands**.
4. **Daniel Ellis, Union Guide:** **Daniel Ellis** of **Carter County**, one of the bridge burners, escaped capture and waged a personal war on the Confederacy. He guided **thousands** of Unionists, draft evaders, escaped POWs, and escaped slaves to Kentucky, earning his living from fees, mail carrying, and stealing Confederate horses. He became a Union cavalry **captain in 1865** and published his memoir in **1867**. He shows how persistent local resistance frustrated the Confederacy in Tennessee.

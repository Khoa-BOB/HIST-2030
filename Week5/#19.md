### Lecture 19: Slavery & the Anti-Slavery Movement in Antebellum Tennessee — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Slavery in Tennessee
* **Enslaved Population (1860):** About **25%** of Tennessee's population — a quarter of the state — was enslaved on the eve of the Civil War, entangling even non-slaveholders in an economy built on enslaved labor.
* **Forced Migration:** From the late 18th century, white settlers brought enslaved people into Tennessee, rupturing family bonds (as recalled by the formerly enslaved woman **Susanna**).
* **Regional Variation:** Life on a large West Tennessee cotton plantation differed sharply from life on a small East Tennessee farm, where few enslaved people worked alongside the white family.

##### Enslaved Women & Community
* **Enslaved Women's Labor:** Expected to perform both domestic work (childcare, cooking, textiles) and field labor, and to step into "men's" jobs when needed.
* **Priscilla Gray:** Formerly enslaved woman from the **Holland household in Williamson County** who recalled weaving, spinning, knitting, and field work.
* **Urban Slavery (Nashville & Memphis):** Enslaved people in cities had somewhat more freedom of movement and could sometimes hire out their labor, though under legal constraints.
* **Beverly Bond:** Historian whose work shows how enslaved women built social networks and community — visiting, trading in markets, secret parties, quilting bees, cornhusking — and resisted family separation.

##### The Anti-Slavery Movement
* **Tennessee Manumission Society (1815):** Umbrella organization of East Tennessee anti-slavery societies; about 1,000 members, nearly all in East Tennessee.
* **Manumission Intelligencer:** One of the first anti-slavery newspapers in the nation, published in Tennessee.
* **Gradual Emancipation & Moral Suasion:** Tennessee's relatively moderate anti-slavery activists petitioned the legislature for gradual emancipation and argued slavery was un-American and un-Christian; the movement lost strength after the **1830s**.
* **Frances "Fanny" Wright & Nashoba:** Scottish-born reformer who founded **Nashoba** (mid-1820s), a colony about 13 miles from Memphis to prepare enslaved people for freedom; she also advocated racial equality, free love, and atheism. After its collapse she transported **31 African Americans to Haiti**.
* **Religious Flip-Flop:** Quakers led early anti-slavery efforts, and Methodists, Baptists, and Presbyterians initially opposed slavery, but by 1830 they modified their stance and by the **1850s** many clergy were staunch defenders of slavery.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Slavery's reach:** With a quarter of the population enslaved, slavery shaped the whole society — even Tennesseans who owned no one lived in an economy built on enslaved labor.
2. **Place shapes experience:** Where an enslaved person lived (plantation, small farm, or city) determined their labor, isolation, and room to maneuver.
3. **Resilience within brutality:** Enslaved people, especially women, forged community and resisted the destruction of family ties, even as the system constantly threatened them.
4. **Anti-slavery's rise and retreat:** Early East Tennessee activism and religious opposition faded after the 1830s, and churches reversed course to defend slavery by the 1850s.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture examines the lives of enslaved people in antebellum Tennessee and the rise and decline of the state's anti-slavery movement.

1. **Slavery's Growth and Reach (late 1700s–1860):** White settlers brought enslaved people into Tennessee from the late 18th century, a forced migration that often tore families apart. By **1860**, roughly **25%** of Tennesseans were enslaved, so even non-slaveholders were bound up in an economy resting on enslaved labor.
2. **Varied Lives Under Slavery:** Experience depended on place: large **West Tennessee cotton plantations** differed from small **East Tennessee farms** where a few enslaved people worked alongside the white family. **Enslaved women** were expected to do both domestic and field labor, as **Priscilla Gray** of **Williamson County** recalled. In **Nashville** and **Memphis**, enslaved people had somewhat more mobility and could sometimes hire out their labor.
3. **Community and Resistance:** Drawing on historian **Beverly Bond**, the lecture stresses how enslaved people built social networks through visiting, market trading, secret parties, quilting bees, and cornhusking. Mothers whose children were sold away sometimes slipped off just to see them, risking being sold further south — evidence of both deep resilience and slavery's brutality.
4. **The Anti-Slavery Movement (1815–1830s):** East Tennessee societies united in the **Tennessee Manumission Society (1815)** and produced the **Manumission Intelligencer**, one of the nation's first anti-slavery papers. Relatively moderate, they petitioned for gradual emancipation using moral suasion, but with only about 1,000 members they lost strength after the 1830s. The most unusual effort was **Frances "Fanny" Wright's Nashoba** colony near Memphis, which collapsed; Wright then transported **31 African Americans to Haiti**.
5. **Religion Reverses Course (1830s–1850s):** Quakers and, initially, Methodists, Baptists, and Presbyterians opposed slavery, but by 1830 the denominations softened their stance and by the **1850s** many ministers had become staunch defenders of slavery — a flip-flop that fed the Southern defense of the institution on the road to the Civil War.

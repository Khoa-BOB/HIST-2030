### Lecture 18: Transportation, Social Class & Culture in Antebellum Tennessee — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### The Transportation Revolution
* **Steamboats:** The first steamboat navigates the **Mississippi River in 1811**, and steamboats reach the **Cumberland River in 1819**, benefiting West Tennessee (Memphis) and Middle Tennessee respectively.
* **Tennessee River:** Natural barriers made it extremely hard to navigate, so the steamboat revolution did little for **East Tennessee**.
* **Railroad Fever:** Enthusiasm begins in the **1830s**, but construction is hampered and does not really take off until the **1850s–1860s**.
* **Louisville & Nashville (L&N) and Memphis & Charleston Railroads:** Key lines of the late 1850s; most lines ran north–south, the Memphis & Charleston being the main exception — so moving goods in and out of the state got easier, but crossing it west-to-east remained hard.
* **Industry:** Some copper mining, iron making, and textile production, but the economy stays primarily agricultural.

##### Social Classes (White Tennessee)
* **Southern Aristocracy / Planter Class:** A small elite with large plantations, professional success, and outsized economic and political power.
* **Yeoman Class:** The large middle rank — small-to-medium landowners (often 50–100 acres), especially numerous in East Tennessee, sometimes owning one or two enslaved people; numerous enough to shape state politics.
* **Poor Whites:** Hired laborers and tenants who lacked property and had very limited economic mobility.
* **Andrew Johnson:** Self-made man born into poverty whose attacks on the "aristocrats" appealed to yeoman class resentment of the planter elite.
* **Free People of Color:** About **7,300 by 1860**; some owned land and pursued socioeconomic mobility despite constrained freedoms.

##### Culture & Class
* **Highbrow Elite Culture:** Private academies, colleges such as **Maryville College**, opera, and celebrity performers like singer **Jenny Lind**.
* **Popular / Backcountry Culture:** Popular literature, **horse racing** (the only acceptable form of gambling), fiddle music, tall-tale storytelling, **shooting matches**, **quilting bees**, and **log rollings** followed by hoedowns, jigs, and square dances.
* **Enslaved People's Secret Parties:** Clandestine gatherings where enslaved people carved out space for socializing and pleasure beyond the master's watch.

##### Honor & Violence
* **Backcountry Fighting & Southern Honor:** Fist fights, brawls, eye-gouging (hence one-eyed men in courthouses), and elite **duels** — all tied to ideals of **manhood and honor** that historians later point to in explaining white Southern mentalities before the Civil War.
* **Tobacco & Whiskey:** Visitors objected to widespread tobacco chewing and whiskey drinking; Middle Tennessee alone had **600+ known stills**.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Geography decides who benefits:** River navigability and north–south rail lines favored Middle and West Tennessee and left East Tennessee — and cross-state travel — behind.
2. **Class consciousness:** A large yeoman class resented the planter elite, giving politicians like **Andrew Johnson** a powerful anti-aristocrat message.
3. **Culture as a class marker:** Elites cultivated highbrow tastes and education, while most Tennesseans — yeomen, poor whites, and enslaved people — shared a backcountry culture of storytelling, music, and communal work-and-play.
4. **Honor and violence:** Backcountry fighting and elite dueling expressed a Southern ideal of never backing down, a mentality later linked to the road to the Civil War.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture continues the survey of antebellum Tennessee, covering the transportation revolution, the class structure of white society, and how culture reflected class.

1. **The Transportation Revolution (1811–1860s):** Steamboats reached the **Mississippi River in 1811** and the **Cumberland River in 1819**, boosting **Memphis** and Middle Tennessee, but the hard-to-navigate **Tennessee River** meant East Tennessee gained little. **Railroad fever** started in the 1830s yet only took off in the **1850s–1860s**; key lines like the **L&N** and **Memphis & Charleston** mostly ran north–south, making it easier to move goods in and out of the state than across it. Some copper mining, iron making, and textile production appeared, but agriculture still dominated.
2. **Social Classes in White Tennessee:** Society divided into a small **planter aristocracy**, a large **yeoman class** of small-to-medium landowners (sometimes holding one or two enslaved people), and a substantial class of **poor whites** working as hired laborers or tenants. Yeoman resentment of the elite fueled politicians like **Andrew Johnson**. About **7,300 free people of color** also lived in the state by 1860, some owning land and seeking mobility despite legal constraints.
3. **Culture & Class:** Elites pursued **highbrow culture** — private academies, colleges like **Maryville College**, and performers such as **Jenny Lind**. Most Tennesseans instead enjoyed popular literature, **horse racing**, fiddle music, tall tales, **shooting matches**, **quilting bees**, and **log rollings** that ended in hoedowns. Enslaved people held **secret parties** to carve out spaces of their own.
4. **Honor, Violence & Vice:** Backcountry Tennessee was known for brawls and eye-gouging fights, while elite men dueled — both rooted in ideals of **manhood and honor** that historians later cite to explain white Southern attitudes on the eve of the Civil War. Visitors also criticized widespread tobacco use and whiskey drinking, with over **600 stills** in Middle Tennessee.

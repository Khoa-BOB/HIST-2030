## A. Anderson Board Votes To Send Group to Capital

**Talks on Bombings Will Be Requested At Earliest Date**

By MAC HARRIS, Staff Correspondent

CLINTON, Tenn. — Anderson county school board voted last night to go to President Eisenhower with its problems growing out of the dynamiting of integrated Clinton high school early Sunday.

**The board, meeting in executive session, voted to send three of its members to Washington to seek a meeting with the President.**

The board issued this terse statement:

"The Anderson county school board has authorized a delegation of Anderson county school officials to seek audience with the President at the earliest possible date to discuss the Clinton high school program."

### Makeup Unknown

James A. Newman, county school superintendent, said the board doesn't know which of its members it will send "or what we will say to the President — we're still working on procedure."

**Without wasting any time, determined teachers and students of the bombed out school earlier in the day went to work whipping a borrowed schoolhouse into shape for occupancy by Thursday as FBI agents took over the investigation of Sunday's dynamitings.**

Francis Moore, chief of the Clinton police force and chairman of the Anderson county school board, said yesterday he turned down offers of assistance in the investigation from police departments in four other southern cities.

### Says FBI Sufficient

"Since the FBI has moved in we don't need their help," Moore said. He said the offers came from Birmingham and Montgomery in Alabama, Jacksonville, Fla., and Nashville.

The high school here, integrated under federal court order in 1956, was left a shambles after three dynamite charges were set off shortly after 4 a.m. Sunday.

**Damage was estimated upward to $300,000 by principal D. W. Human.**

A team of at least four FBI agents was actively investigating the dynamiting, probing wreckage and questioning persons who lived near the school.

The only clues reported thus far are two six-inch fragments of spent fuse and fragments of textbooks bearing the odor of an explosive. These were found in the debris by Walter Bearden, agent for Tennessee Bureau of Criminal Identification.

### Clues To Be Examined

Bearden said yesterday these will be sent to the FBI laboratories for examination.

## B. McCord Pledges To Keep Open State's Schools

**Former Governor Announces, To Seek Runoff Law**

By LEE WINFREY

Jim Nance McCord announced for governor yesterday and pledged that if elected he will keep the public schools of Tennessee open.

**"Responsibility to the youth of Tennessee demands open public schools," McCord said. "While my whole background reflects the philosophy of segregation of the races, the public school system of this state must continue to grow, uninterrupted by lawlessness and violence."**

The 79-year-old McCord, governor from 1944 to 1948, announced his candidacy at a press conference called at 4 p.m. yesterday in his suite at the James Robertson hotel. He said he was running as a Democrat and considered education the major issue of the campaign.

### Direct Conflict

McCord's position on keeping the schools open places him in direct conflict with Buford Ellington, the Democratic nominee for governor.

**Ellington, who won nomination in August with slightly more than 31 per cent of the vote, said during his campaign that he would close Tennessee's schools, if necessary, to prevent violence.**

McCord and Ellington served together in the cabinet of Gov. Frank G. Clement, McCord as commissioner of conservation and Ellington as commissioner of agriculture.

McCord resigned from Clement's cabinet June 26 and threw his support to Judge Andrew T. (Tip) Taylor of Jackson, who finished second to Ellington in the primary. It was reported at the time that McCord had sought the nod as the administration candidate, but that Clement had told him that he was too old.

### Defines Campaign

Yesterday, McCord took great pains to define his campaign as in the nature of a run-off election. In a prepared statement read to about 20 newsmen assembled in his suite, he said:

"I am announcing that I have responded to the wishes of more than two thirds of the Democratic voters of Tennessee whose majority will was denied in the recent Democratic primary. ## 1. Students Angered But Morale High

**Clinton High Prepares To Take Up Work in Borrowed School**

CLINTON, Tenn. — The dynamite scarred shell of a school building, a slightly damaged gymnasium and a football field awaited the students who trooped back to Clinton high school yesterday.

And they didn't like it.

"It was pretty dirty, what they did," said Lana Underwood, a 14-year-old freshman. "Things were confusing enough anyway, for a freshman. And now we don't even have a school to go to."

Lana was one of 870 Clinton high school students who was left without a school, after dynamiters all but destroyed their plant Sunday morning.

### Regular Hours Today

Yesterday they assembled at 8:30 a.m. for less than three hours of activities and a smattering of classroom work before being sent home by Principal W. D. Human.

Today and tomorrow they will be at school for the regular hours, although little academic work is planned. By Thursday, Human hopes to have moved the school to Oak Ridge, six miles away, where a borrowed elementary school will be mustered into use to take care of the emergency.

"It's no fun going to school without a school building," said Nevie Barton, 16, one of the 11 Negro students enrolled at the school. **"Everything was going all right until this happened. But none of the white students have tried to blame it on us. I don't think there is any resentment."**

Barton's statement was pretty well borne out by Charles Smith, 18, senior. "I'll put it this way," he said, "we don't care for Negroes being in our school, but when they start dynamiting the school it's going too far.

"We are not blaming the Negroes for it. But now, I don't know where I'll get a diploma from. I want to be a civil engineer and I don't think I'll get the education I should. We had a lot of good science equipment that was blown up. Now we've got nothing."

**Teachers met with the principal 45 minutes before the students came into the auditorium yesterday morning, and Human said they all volunteered to do whatever they could to keep the school going.**

"Most of them called me Sunday and offered to help," Human said. "They are sticking together. Sometimes a crisis brings people closer together."

That is apparently what happened here, as students and faculty members joined together to get Linden school ready in Oak Ridge.

The boys and girls at Clinton gave Human an ovation when he entered the gymnasium yesterday morning to speak to them. Later, after the assembly was dismissed, they went in groups to various activities or simply wandered around the grounds.

William Peters, a new history teacher, quizzed his class on the Far Eastern situation. They used a stone wall for seats.

**"The students don't like what happened," Peters said. "But morale is high and everybody is helping out 100 per cent."**

Today, students will spend a regular school day doing such things as practicing band and joining in intramural sports.

Human said they won't attempt too much class work.

Although all of the students knew what had happened to their school, Sunday, the full significance failed to sink in until they arrived for classes yesterday.

"We are all working as a team," said Mrs. Sidney Davis, a teacher who spent the afternoon yesterday at Oak Ridge helping to get Linden school ready. "Everybody is co-operating."

. . . the board for the apparent purpose of deciding whether to build a new high school and use the old one as a junior high.

**Officials indicated yesterday that this is probably what will be done here since Clinton high was already overcrowded.**

Members of the board are O. C. Mayes and Ralph Shanlever, Clinton; Dr. J. M. Cox, Lake City; Mayor R. G. Crossno of Norris; Charles Phillips, Devonia, and J. M. Sisson, Oliver Springs.

### Students Go To Work

While tight-lipped FBI agents questioned residents of the high school area yesterday, 18 students and most of the school's 32 teachers rolled up their sleeves and went to work at Linden elementary school in nearby Oak Ridge, getting it ready for the 870 Clinton students by Thursday.

**Human, the principal, said Thursday was the target date for transferring to the government-owned 26-room schoolhouse that officials of Oak Ridge offered to Anderson county yesterday.**

Students were assembled in the Clinton high gymnasium yesterday at 8:30 a.m. Some classes were held, but mostly the students joined in intramural activities or simply wandered around the school grounds, staring at the wreckage left by Sunday morning's dynamiters.

### Pep Rally Held

Several groups of students were sent out to play football and six pretty girls practiced school cheers near their roped-off school building. Other students held a giant pep rally in the gymnasium and sang their school "fight song."

**"This crisis has drawn students and the teachers together," Human said. He said the 31 teachers all called him Sunday and told him they would continue at "any sacrifice."**

G. C. Gearty, FBI agent in charge of the Knoxville office who is heading the investigation, had little to say about the federal government's role in tracking down the dynamiters.

### Says He Is 'Assisting'

"I was sent in here to assist local authorities," Gearty said, "by making available to them the facilities of our laboratories and identification facilities, and to run down out of state leads that might develop."

Gearty, whose home is in Knoxville, admitted he would be on call in Clinton at any time, although he would not say that his headquarters had been set up there.

"As for the investigation, I had rather not comment," he said.

**It was no secret to Clinton residents that FBI agents were going from door to door in the vicinity of the school, asking questions and trying to find leads to the baffling case.**

The FBI was ordered into the investigation yesterday afternoon by J. Edgar Hoover, director.

At least four agents were seen in Clinton Sunday, and probably more were there. As the probe went on, students were kept clear of the main school building by county officers who have been on 24-hour duty around the shattered school since the blasts. The three explosions, which came at three-minute intervals starting at 4:21 a.m. destroyed most of the interior of the building, but surprisingly left the front facade, built in 1927, intact.

### Integration To Continue

Human said that classes will continue on an integrated basis in Clinton until the school at Oak Ridge is made available for occupancy.

**Moore said that the county carried only $73,000 worth of insurance on the school.**

"Our insurance won't cover a third of the damage," said Newman, the superintendent.

The explosions ripped out walls, scattered desks, steel lockers, tables around like a hurricane and laid a carpet of splintered glass around the outside of the building.

Walter Bearden, agent for the Tennessee Bureau of Criminal Identification, said he found slivers of glass and wood embedded in walls inside the building. "It looked like a hurricane had hit," Bearden said.

### Governor Pledges Help

Gov. Frank Clement, who called Clinton officials to an emergency conference in Nashville yesterday, has promised the full co-operation of the state in solving the dynamitings and helping local official repair the damage.

Human said Sunday that the county's 10 busses and as many "extra" busses as needed would be used to transport students to Oak Ridge when the school there is made ready.

"In the meantime," the school principal said, "we are going to carry on school here. The P-TA and cafeteria personnel will have sandwiches for the students. They will be served in the gymnasium."

**Human said that the city elementary school of Clinton, the city's recreation building and the national guard armory will be used to hold classes in until Linden school is made ready in Oak Ridge.**

"We will of course concentrate on outside activities rather than classroom studies until we move," Human said. "But school is not going to stop."

### Safeguards Prepared

At Oak Ridge yesterday, students and teachers were busy hauling out junior-size desks and chairs so that equipment large enough for high school students could be installed.

Newman said "safeguards will be taken" at the borrowed school to prevent a recurrence of the disaster which struck Clinton high.

"We will have the grounds guarded," Newman said. He added that Clement had offered the use of state help if needed.

Anderson county Judge J. D. Yarnell supervised the work at the Oak Ridge school.

**"We've got students, teachers, county crew and men we have just picked up helping us," Yarnell said.**

"We are very pleased with the facility Oak Ridge has let us use. Actually, it is better than the one we had in Clinton."

Clinton has been the scene of recurrent dynamitings since 1956, when the school first opened its doors to 12 Negro students.

### 9 Prior Blasts Counted

At least nine explosions had been reported here before Sunday morning's devastating climax.

Clinton high had 10 Negro students registered for classes at the start of this school year. Three others registered later. And two subsequently left school, leaving 11 Negro students enrolled now.

It had been previously reported that there were only nine Negro students in the school.

**All but one of the 11 Negro students reported to the gymnasium for classes yesterday. Principal Human said that about 90 per cent of the white students were present.**

Meanwhile, Human promised students that the school's football schedule would not be interrupted and that the band would journey to Bristol, Tenn., Saturday for a musical festival as planned.

## 3. Human Takes Blow To School in Stride

By BILL RAWLINS

CLINTON, Tenn. —(AP)— The man with the job of keeping Clinton high school alive despite the explosions which shattered its building has shoulders broad enough for the task.

Almost everyone agrees he's putting them to the wheel.

**W. D. Human, in his second year as principal of the school, has been an educator for 28 years — including four years in the army in World War II. He came out a captain, and got right back into the teaching profession.**

He's married and his daughter Linda, 10, is in the fifth grade at Clinton elementary school. Human taught the chairman of the Anderson county school board, which hired him, in high school. His surname is apt.

Human stepped in as Clinton principal after the high school already was integrated, and with jut-jawed determination that he would have none of the harrassment that plagued his predecessor, D. J. Brittain Jr.

Brittain resigned in 1957, after the initial year of race-mixing at the school, a year frought with violence and agonizing problems for a man of Brittain's sensitive temperament. He accepted a teaching fellowship at New York university to work on his doctor's degree.

"I finished high school at Coalfield (Tenn.) under Mr. Human," said Francis Moore, Clinton police chief and chairman of the school board. "He was my principal and he taught me mathematics. That was in 1936 and 1937. I knew he was very strict but very fair, and as far as I could ever tell, without any kind of prejudice against anyone.

"I was on the school board when we needed to hire a successor to Mr. Brittain, and I knew that Mr. Human was the kind of a man we needed here."

Most of Clinton seems to agree.

### Ready for Opening

Human, who had been superintendent of schools in neighboring Morgan county for 10 years, took over his duties in the summer of 1957. When school opened on an integrated basis for the second year Human was ready. He met the school busses bringing the students to school, and ushered the youngsters quickly inside the school. Nobody was allowed to congregate outside — student or adult. Human took charge early — and he has kept that charge even through Sunday's dynamiting.

**"Mr. Human is a good principal," said the mother of a student in the school. "He has discipline, and he has the respect of his students. The children like him."**

Human is a modest man with a greying crew-cut, warm black eyes and a ready smile. In his school, his word is law — but he obviously is liked, not feared, by the students.

The school's destruction came as a blow, but Human took it standing up.

"No school man likes to see his plant blown to bits," he related.

**"Morale is our biggest problem," he said, after the explosion. "We have to keep the kids together; to give them a sense of togetherness so that they will realize that we're going forward in spite of what happened."**

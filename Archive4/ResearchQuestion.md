# Response;

For this assignment I studied how the *Nashville Tennessean* covered the dynamiting of Clinton High School before dawn on Sunday, October 5, 1958. Every white person quoted condemned the bombing, and several, including the governor, said they had never wanted integration.

I searched for "Clinton high school" and "Clinton bombing." I chose a single school because I wanted one Tennessee event I could follow from day to day. Adding "bombing" took me straight to the October attack.

I used three front-page stories from Monday, October 6, under the banner "Bombs Wreck Clinton High; Classes To Resume Today." In "3 Blasts Rip Classrooms, Walls, Halls," Mac Harris called Clinton High "the first public school in Tennessee to desegregate classrooms" (fall 1956), reported blasts starting at 4:21 a.m., and quoted Police Chief Francis Moore: "There's no doubt this dynamiting is connected with integration of the school." In "Dynamite Can't Replace 3 R's, Clement Declares," Governor Frank Clement promised to keep the school open but said, "There is not a man in this room trying to promote integration." In "Students Stunned, Irate, Elders Shocked, Alarmed," student council president Calvin Hill said, "We did not want to integrate, but there is not one of us, I know, who believes in fighting it with dynamite."

From Tuesday, October 7, I used two stories. Mac Harris's "Anderson Board Votes To Send Group to Capital," continued on page 2 as "Clinton Seeks President Aid," reported that the school board voted to ask President Eisenhower for help and that classes would stay integrated. In "Students Angered But Morale High," senior Charles Smith said, "we don't care for Negroes being in our school, but when they start dynamiting the school it's going too far."

I did not find a named suspect or any segregationist group. Nevie Barton, 16, quoted in "Students Angered," was the only Black student quoted on either day. The October 6 paper counted nine Black students; "Clinton Seeks President Aid" corrected it to 11.

These pages suggest that Tennessee's public argument in 1958 was about order more than equality. Officials and students agreed that dynamite went too far, and Anderson County's answer was practical: obey the court order, keep the school open, and ask Washington for help. By that Thursday, Principal W. D. Human planned to reopen Clinton High, still integrated, in a borrowed building in Oak Ridge.

## A. 3 Blasts Rip Classrooms, Walls, Halls

**Damage to Million-Dollar School Estimated $100,000 to $300,000; Churches, Oak Ridge School Offered**

By MAC HARRIS, Staff Correspondent

CLINTON, Tenn. — Three explosions shattered Clinton high school early yesterday morning causing an estimated $100,000 to $300,000 damage and leaving 850 students without classrooms to go to this morning.

**The first of the three blasts, which left most of the million-dollar county school plant in shambles, ripped through the brick and concrete block structure at 4:21 a.m. while most of Clinton slept.**

Three minutes later a second explosion occured, followed by a third at 4:27 a.m. The final explosion wrecked three classrooms, cracked an outside brick wall, ripped through the floor and played havoc in the school cafeteria below.

### State's Resources Offered

Gov. Frank G. Clement, who labeled the blast a "cowardly act," and Clinton officials announced in Nashville by late afternoon that all the resources of the state and the county will be used to operate a high school.

R. G. Crossno, Anderson county school board member, vowed the school will be operated as it has been. He said Clinton high students will assemble at the gymnasium at 8:30 a.m. today and will be told where to receive instructions until the school is repaired.

### Churches Offer Facilities

He said churches have volunteered all their facilities. Too, Oak Ridge, Tenn., officials announced they will make a 25-room school there available to the Clinton school board for the displaced students.

Clement asked the officials to fly to Nashville for the afternoon conference.

**Police Chief Francis Moore, also the county school board chairman, said:**

**"There's no doubt this dynamiting is connected with integration of the school. It was a professional job."**

Moore said the school cost $750,000 when it was built 30 years ago to accommodate 640 students. There have been additions since, raising the value to $1,000,000. Some insurance was carried on the building but he didn't know the exact amount.

It was just two years ago that Clinton high school under federal court order opened its doors to Negro students, thus becoming the first public school in Tennessee to desegregate classrooms. That was the fall of 1956. The violence and riots which followed rocked this normally peaceful East Tennessee town and were only quelled after Clement ordered the national guard here.

Dudley Human, principal of the school, surveyed the wreckage with dismay yesterday.

### Called Complete Shock

"This is a complete shock," he said. "This year we have had no trouble. We had a few little incidents among students last year, but nothing of major importance."

## B. Dynamite Can't Replace 3 R's, Clement Declares

**Pledges All State Help To Keep Schools Open**

By EUGENE DIETZ

All state and Anderson county resources will be used to keep the integrated Clinton high school open, Gov. Frank G. Clement and key Anderson county officials agreed here yesterday.

**"We don't intend to let three sticks of dynamite replace the three R's," Clement said after a conference.**

"We have a choice of education by democracy or education by dynamite," Clement said.

The governor, who asked five Anderson county officials to fly here aboard an air national guard plane for the conference in the governor's mansion late yesterday, told newsmen following the meeting:

"There is not a man in this room trying to promote integration. But everyone here (the Anderson county officials were still in the room) believes in law and order. We are determined that law and order shall prevail."

### 'Will Maintain Law'

Asked by a reporter if that meant the national guard will be used in Clinton if necessary, Clement said: "Whatever it takes to maintain law and order we will use."

Attending the 45-minute conference at the governor's mansion on Curtiswood lane were: County Judge J. D. Yarnell of Anderson county; Dudley Human, principal of Clinton high school; J. A. Newman, Anderson county superintendent of schools; Francis Moore, who serves in the dual capacity of Clinton police chief and chairman of the Anderson county school board, and R. G. Crossno, Anderson county school board member. Crossno also is mayor of Norris, Tenn., 11 miles from Clinton.

Crossno, who spoke on behalf of the entire Anderson county delegation in a 6 p.m. news conference in the governor's mansion, said that Clinton high school will be operated "on the same basis as the past."

He said that Clinton students will assemble at the gymnasium this morning and will be given instructions on where to go until the school is repaired.

Yarnell, asked if there were a possibility that the county might attempt to end integration which had been ordered by federal court, replied:

**"No. It is my opinion that we are still under the federal court order. We shall continue to operate just as we have."**

## C. Students Stunned, Irate, Elders Shocked, Alarmed

CLINTON, Tenn. — Student leaders at Clinton high school were as stunned and outraged as their elders yesterday to the news that their school had been bombed.

**As an early morning fog lifted slowly, brushed aside by a hot Indian summer sun, Clinton students, their parents and officials looked with disbelief at the dynamite-wrecked school.**

Calvin Hill, president of the high school's student council, said:

"The students are shocked that someone would actually blow up our school. Personally, I couldn't believe it at first. Everything's been going real well this year. We haven't had any open opposition to the colored students. There's maybe been a spot or two, but that could happen between anybody. I am not afraid of anyone trying to harm us, but they sure did harm our school. In fact, they abolished it."

### All Will Obey Law

"We did not want to integrate, but there is not one of us, I know, who believes in fighting it with dynamite," Hill said.

Dale Keeney, president of the senior class, said: "The dynamiting was a deliberate attempt to cause trouble without any relationship to what was going on inside the school.

"We have operated efficiently, without a bit of trouble, for two years now. There is not a person in our school who would sympathize with the dynamiters.

"The only way to work this out is to abide by the federal court order and to support the law. And every student in the senior class will obey the law, I am sure, for two reasons: first, out of respect for the law and then because we want to graduate."

Hill said, "The student council will do everything we can to obey the law and co-operate with our faculty. The student body is too shocked to support any other position. They will co-operate."

### Other Students Agree

These were the reactions of other Clinton students reached yesterday:

Jimmy Rhyne, 18, senior: "We don't like it a bit. We'll have to go to another school now."

## 1. Fuse Lone Clue In Dynamiting

**Fragments Slated for Laboratory Test as FBI Agents Enter Case**

By STEVE KORPAN and CHARLES COATES

Authorities by last night had found a single clue they hoped will help solve the Clinton high school dynamiting — fragments of a dynamite fuse.

W. E. (Bud) Hopton, director of Tennessee's bureau of criminal identification, said the fragments were found in debris at the school.

He said they will be submitted to laboratory tests in an effort to find a telltale clue of some kind that will lead to the source and put the investigation that much closer to the dynamiter or dynamiters.

FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover announced in Washington that federal agency has entered the investigation and has extended to local Tennessee authorities the full facilities of the FBI laboratory.

He said that G. C. Gearty, agent in charge of the Knoxville FBI office, has arrived at Clinton to take charge "of all investigative efforts that may come within the jurisdiction of the FBI."

Alden Tillers, Rockwood police chief, said he will resume his investigation of the possibility of a dynamite theft there two weeks ago. A magazine which stores dynamite from two companies, Tennessee Products and Roane Electrical company, was burglarized.

Tillers said the chemical company had said its dynamite stores were untouched but that no report had been received from the other firm.

Police Chief Douglas Hosse said in Nashville that integrated schools here are "under observation."

Yesterday's Clinton blast recalled similar explosions here when newly integrated Hattie Cotton school was damaged in September, 1957, and Jewish Community center last March 16. Neither bombing has been solved.

### Clues Few

The only clues in the community center blast, Nashville Detective Lt. Rex White said here, are a fragment of a fuse and the report of a car speeding from the scene shortly before the explosion ripped the front of the building. Nobody was hurt in either blast.

There are now no Negro children in Hattie Cotton school.

On the legislative front, Rep. J. Carlton Loser said yesterday he will try again to push through congress legislation tightening controls on dynamite.

Hopton and White agreed yesterday that major bombings in cities in the Southeast apparently have been the work "of some organized group because the bombings follow a pattern and occur one right after the other."

"We'll throw everything we have into this investigation," Hopton vowed. "We've already started our work and have found a fragment of a fuse. And we know there were three separate dynamite explosions, at 4:20 a.m., 4:24 a.m. and at 4:27 a.m."

Speculation was that the dynamite came from one of the numerous mining operations in that area and that it might have been Coalite, a substance similar to dynamite.

"We don't know where it came from," said Police Chief Francis Moore, also the school board chairman. "With all the mines operating near here it could have come from here. Or it could have been slipped in from outside."

### Nothing New

Dynamiting in Clinton is nothing new. A series of eight blasts there in 1956, following integration of the high school, was the impetus behind a new state dynamite law passed by the 1957 state assembly.

This law buffered existing laws by making it unlawful to possess dynamite or detonating equipment except for a lawful purpose.

Principal effect of the new law, according to Harry G. Nichol, Davidson county district attorney general, is to shift responsibility for proving lawful possession from the state to the possessor.

The new law also made unlawful possession punishable by imprisonment of 2 to 10 years rather than fine only, as under previous laws.

### Sales Restricted

Tennessee law also restricts the sale of dynamite and requires registration of sales with the state fire marshal.

"But," said Hopton, "although not just every Tom, Dick and Harry can buy dynamite, the law doesn't prevent them from stealing it."

On the national level, Rep. Loser said last night he will re-introduce a bill enabling the Federal Bureau of Investigation to aid state authorities in investigation of dynamitings.

"I am extremely hopeful it will pass in the next session of congress (convening in January). This (yesterday's dynamiting) will undoubtedly add more momentum to the bill, which was favorably reported by a subcommittee at the close of the last session."

On the Nashville bombings, Hopton said, "We're still checking any possible leads we may get. We've questioned many persons and will continue our investigations but we haven't sufficient evidence to arrest anyone yet."

He declined to comment further.

Nashville's two main blasts, aside from multiple explosion cases reported in strikebound business houses and firms where violence attended union picket activities, involved:

- Hattie R. Cotton school, 1033 Greenwood ave., where a 12:33 a.m. explosion Sept. 10, 1957, caused $71,000 damage and shattered residential windows for blocks.
- Jewish community center, 3500 West End ave., where a similar but smaller blast caused $6000 damages at 8:07 p.m. March 16.

The latter blast was followed by anonymous phone calls threatening the life of U.S. District Court Judge W. E. Miller, whose home and person for a long time was guarded by federal agents and private police, and threat of destruction of The Temple, Harding road synagogue, where Dr. William B. Silverman is rabbi.

After the community center blast here Nashville police met with authorities from Alabama and Florida at Jacksonville, Fla., at the request of Jacksonville's mayor Hayden Burns.

Jacksonville has no integrated schools but the James Weldon Johnson junior high school was dynamited several months ago.

Burns said that when the Jacksonville conference was held, "We decided to set up a system for disseminating such information that we might have to other areas. We will make our information available to Clinton."

Both Hopton and White emphasized that organization of some sort is apparently behind the dynamitings, pointing to the fact that the community center bombing here followed only by hours the explosion at a Miami Jewish community center where $30,000 damage was caused and by report of an attempted dynamiting a short time later at a Birmingham church. Only Police Chief D. E. Hosse doesn't "think this is any connection" between the various bombings.

Police attributed the Hattie Cotton school bombing to action by segregation extremists, emphasizing that a Negro child was registered for attendance there at the start of the school semester in 1957. The child attended for one day only.

The community center bombing yielded "a large fragment of the fuse," White continued. Here police believed the bombing was caused by extreme racialism.

. . . sat down to the task of deciding how to go on teaching without a school. The student body of approximately 850 includes nine Negro students.

**The FBI also joined the investigation. The only clue this far were dynamite fuse fragments found at the scene.**

Church congregations meeting only a few hours after the disaster offered to let classes meet in their Sunday school buildings until the high school could be readied for occupancy — a task that officials say may take months. The biggest boost to distraught school authorities was the Oak Ridge offer.

"We don't like it when people interfere with our schools," said L. P. Cushman, superintendent of schools at Oak Ridge. "If we can do anything at all to help, we intend to. There is no need for Clinton children to miss school."

### Request Expected Today

Cushman said that Linden school in Oak Ridge would be made available within 24 hours after a formal request is received from school officials in Clinton.

Frederick Ford, director of community affairs for the Atomic Energy agency at Oak Ridge, said he met with school officials yesterday.

"They said this was an emergency and requested the use of Linden school. We anticipate a formal request Monday morning."

Linden school was closed two years ago, Cushman said, when hundreds of construction workers and their families moved out of the area. Since then it has been maintained on a 24-hour standby basis.

The Clinton dynamiting was the second blast in Tennessee to damage an integrated school. A year ago last September the Hattie Cotton Elementary school in Nashville was damaged heavily by a similar explosion. It came shortly after the first grade of some of Nashville's city schools was integrated.

The one Negro student there was withdrawn after the blast. Hattie Cotton has no Negro students now.

### Clement Notes 5 Steps

Clement announced at the Nashville conference with Clinton officials in the Curtiswood lane mansion that he had taken five steps to assure opening of school today for the youngsters displaced by dynamiting.

He said these were:

- Holding of the fact-finding meeting.
- Full investigation by Tennessee Bureau of Criminal Identification.
- Reward of $5,000 for information leading to arrest and conviction of the dynamiters.
- Use of state facilities that might aid in solving the "serious problems."
- Sending of a special representative, Wesley Pickel, director of school plant construction division of the state education department, to Clinton.

(Mayor Haydon Burns of Jacksonville, Florida, told Associated Press that a $55,700 reward offered earlier this year will be paid also for information leading to solution of yesterday's Clinton bombing.

(The reward was pledged at a conference of officials from Southern cities May 3 after a series of dynamiting incidents.)

### Main Building Damaged

Almost all of the main building of Clinton high was damaged by the early morning blasts which came as a heavy fog obscured vision.

Walter Bearden, TBI agent from Knoxville in charge of the investigation, said one blast was set off near the boiler, demolishing that room, and an entrance way that led to the school gymnasium. The blast ripped through a wall to an adjacent science laboratory, wrecking the room and $8,000 worth of new science equipment.

**Another charge tore out a wall in the center of the building and wrecked several classrooms. Debris was hurled into the school auditorium. Bearden said he discovered two small pieces of spent dynamite fuse in the corridor of the south wing, scene of the third explosion. These pieces of fuse, along with torn books, heavily scented with an odor Bearden says came from dynamite, will be sent to the FBI laboratories in Washington for analysis.**

"We won't be able to determine too much until some of this mess is cleared up and we can get in closer," Bearden said.

He said that hanging masonry and damaged floors made it dangerous at this time to fully examine the spots where the charges were set off.

Nobody would give an exact estimate of the damage, but Principal Human said it would probably run from $100,000 to $300,000.

### Too Early To Estimate

"It's just too early to tell yet," Human said. "We don't know how much structural damage has been done."

The gymnasium, a home economics section, the band room, and the library were the only parts of the school which escaped damage.

Both Bearden and Human said the explosives were placed so as to do the maximum amount of damage to the building. Surprisingly enough there was no damage to any of the houses near the school or to the gymnasium, 50 feet from the main building.

**In Nashville, where Hattie Cotton school and the Jewish Community Center were dynamited, and in Miami and Jacksonville, where similar bombings occurred, explosives were set outside the building or in entrance ways. This was also true in Birmingham where a bag of unexploded dynamite was found against the wall of a school.**

Police officials from these and other Southern communities who met in Jacksonville to try and work out strategy to combat bombers stated publicly that such dynamiting was apparently more for show and to cause intimidation than to cause severe damage.

"Whoever did this job (at Clinton) knew what he was doing and was out to cause as much damage as he could," said Asst. Police Chief Jesse Braden.

### Had Odor of Colite

Braden said that from the odor inside the building the bombers apparently used colite, a slow-burning explosive commonly used in the coal mines in this section.

"There's plenty of it around here and it would be easy for somebody to steal some," the assistant chief said. He estimated several cases were used inside the school.

"I think a man who would do something like this ought to be blown up himself," Braden said.

In nearby Rockwood, Tenn., Police Chief Alden Tiller was checking out a report there of a powder magazine being broken into two or three months ago.

"So far as I can learn," Tiller said, "there were no explosives stolen, but we are still checking on it."

Braden said that only eight days before the school was dynamited a small explosion was set off on Foley hill, the Negro section of Clinton two blocks from the high school.

"That one was set off on a cedar post between two houses," Braden said. "It didn't hurt anybody. One of the houses was vacant and an old man and his daughter lived in the other one."

### Blast Damages Homes

In February, 1957, Foley hill was rocked by an earlier explosion, damaging a score of homes, wrecking a cafe, and injuring two persons.

Clinton high school is just across Hicks street from the city's combined police and fire station. Braden said that two patrolmen, Curtis Roseberry and John Haunshell, were on duty yesterday morning and got the exact time of the blasts, but saw nothing.

"There was so much fog you couldn't see 10 feet away," he said. "And whoever did was probably long gone before the first charge went off."

Bearden, the TBI agent, speculated that the bombers entered the building from the rear, set the explosives to go off at the same time, and then fled. He said they could have used fuses long enough to have given them ample time to get away from the scene before the charges went off.

County and city police roped off the school grounds yesterday and the thousands of sightseers who jammed the street outside the school were not allowed on the grounds.

### 2 Extra Deputies Hired

Anderson county Sheriff Glad Woodward, sick in bed yesterday, said he had put on two extra deputies to work with his 10 regular deputies in patroling the school tonight. Workers erected huge floodlights outside the school yesterday and they were left burning last night.

Even teachers who came to view the wreckage of their classrooms were shooed away yesterday.

"This is a terrible thing, I don't know what they'll do next," said one teacher who asked not to be identified.

**James A. Newman, county school superintendent, ordered a faculty meeting at 7:45 a.m. today at the school gymnasium. He issued a call to students to report to the gymnasium at 8:30 a.m. Newman, Police Chief Francis Moore, and other Clinton officials flew to Nashville yesterday for a hurried conference with Governor Clement. They were promised the full cooperation of the state, including the use of national guardsmen, if needed.**

Congregations at St. Mark's and Memorial Methodist churches and the First Baptist church took prompt action yesterday morning in offering their facilities for the students to use.

"I think the people of this town and the churches will do everything they can to help out," said F. C. Potter, county supervisor of teachers.

# Response

**Film:** *At the River I Stand* (1993), documentary on the 1968 Memphis sanitation strike

**1. How is Memphis portrayed?**

The film shows Memphis as a city that had integrated its public spaces but kept its economy segregated. The narrator says that in the 1960s Memphis avoided the kind of uprisings seen in Little Rock, Birmingham and Selma by quietly integrating movies, libraries and lunch counters (2:03), but Black workers were still stuck in the lowest-paid jobs. The sanitation department is the clearest example. One worker describes it as a job "beneath the level of a White man," a "social caste" (2:26), and says the men carried leaking tubs of garbage and had to strip off their clothes at the door when they got home because maggots had fallen on them (2:37–2:52). There was no overtime, no sick leave, and a man hurt on the job could be fired. Pay was so low that full-time workers still qualified for welfare (3:00–3:05).

The second picture of Memphis is a city run by people who thought they knew what was best for Black residents. A white Memphian interviewed in the film calls Mayor Henry Loeb's attitude "plantation psychology," the idea that "we own the plantation, they work on the plantation" (6:41–6:55). Later another white interviewee admits that "almost everybody stood rock solid that we White people knew what was best for those Black people" (21:27). The city government backs this up with force. When marchers leave City Hall after the council breaks its promise, police push their cars into the crowd and then use mace and nightsticks (14:13–14:40). After the March 28 march falls apart on Beale Street, Loeb calls in the National Guard and puts the whole city under a 7 p.m. curfew (34:31–35:18).

The film also shows a third Memphis: the Black community organizing itself. Churches pass buckets instead of offering plates to fund the strike (17:06), people sign up to picket and answer phones (16:43), and the "I am a man" signs appear on the picket line (18:29–18:45).

**2. Subject matter, approach, and what it reveals**

The subject is the 1968 sanitation workers' strike. It started after two workers, Echol Cole and Robert Walker, were crushed to death by a faulty garbage compressor in February 1968, and their families got no compensation or insurance (4:59–5:10). About 1,300 men walked off the job. The strike lasted 63 days, drew Martin Luther King Jr. to Memphis, and ended shortly after his assassination with the city recognizing the union (53:38, 55:37).

The film tells the story mostly through the people who were there. There's no host. Instead it cuts between strikers, union officials, ministers, members of the Invaders, King's staff, and even white Memphians who supported Loeb, and puts their interviews next to news footage from 1968. That choice lets the people involved disagree on camera. For example, the Invaders say they were shut out of planning because "Black power" made them look like a threat (29:10), and one of them argues that nonviolence was "a tactic and a tactic alone" (29:58–30:03). The narration fills in the gaps between these voices, and gospel and freedom songs ("One more river to cross," "Keep your mind set on freedom") carry the story from one chapter to the next.

What it reveals about Memphis and Tennessee is that civil rights and economic rights were the same fight. Desegregating lunch counters didn't change who hauled the garbage. Loeb's position rested on Tennessee law, since public employees weren't allowed to strike (6:31), and he refused to recognize the union for the whole 63 days (53:06). King made the economic point directly in his March 18 speech, telling the crowd that "it is a crime for people to live in this rich nation and receive starvation wages" (25:09–25:40). The film argues that Memphis was where the movement shifted from integration to poverty, and that the strike was the bridge to King's Poor People's Campaign, which left for Washington from the Lorraine Motel two weeks after the strike was settled (55:55–56:11).

The film left me angry at Memphis's leaders and moved by its ordinary people. Even more than King's death, what got to me was how small the workers' demands were. They wanted a raise, a grievance procedure and a union, and the city answered with mace, the National Guard and a curfew. The scene at the council meeting where a man asks, "Did I insult you by telling the truth?" and the council just votes to adjourn (19:18–19:40) made the city government feel cold and stubborn. The ending changed that feeling a bit. When the men vote to accept the settlement, the child of a strike leader remembers their father crying while holding a telegram from Vice President Hubert Humphrey (54:38–55:13), and one worker says, "We lost many things, but we stayed and we've got the victory" (54:31). The Memphis I came away with is a city whose government failed its workers badly, but whose Black community won anyway by sticking together for 63 days.

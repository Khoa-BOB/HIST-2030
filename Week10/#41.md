### Lecture 41: Creating the Great Smoky Mountains National Park — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### The Place
* **The Smokies:** Part of the southern Appalachians, extending into North Carolina and South Carolina, with some of the highest mountains in the eastern U.S. *[Correction: the Smokies lie in Tennessee and North Carolina, not South Carolina.]*
* **Biodiversity:** The park covers over **500,000 acres**. The peaks get about 80 inches of rain a year, with spruce forest at the top and hardwoods with dogwood and rhododendron below.

##### Human Influence Before Logging
* **Cherokee:** The lecture calls them the first inhabitants. They farmed the valleys, burned the woods for pest control and game management, and hunted more intensively after contact with Europeans. *[Correction: earlier Native peoples came before the Cherokee; see Week 1.]*
* **White Settlers:** North Carolina land grants drew German, English, and Scottish settlers, including John Jacob and Sarah Mingus. They saw the forest as dark and threatening. *[Transcript: "John Jacob and Sarah Minh." "Mingus" is my best reading: John Jacob Mingus is a documented early settler, but I could not confirm the German origin or land grant for him.]*
* **Predator Bounties:** Tennessee paid **$6** per wolf pelt and **$1** per red fox pelt. As predators declined, squirrels, crows, possums, raccoons, and pigeons thrived.
* **Squirrel and Crow Tax:** The General Assembly required adult men to pay a tax in squirrel or crow scalps. *[Transcript: "scouts."]*
* **Passenger Pigeon:** Heavily shot, including by residents of Pigeon Forge. It is now extinct.

##### Logging
* **Lumber Industry:** A New South industry drawn by old-growth forest, cheap land, new technology, and railroads. Per **Margaret Brown**, it clearcut **60%** (around **300,000 acres**) of the Smokies in 20 years, removing about **2 billion board feet**.
* **Champion:** A major company whose plant's effluent turned the **Pigeon River** "as dark as tea."
* **Conservation vs. Preservation:** The **Forest Service** saw forests as a resource to manage scientifically. Preservationists wanted them left alone.
* **Ecological and Cultural Loss:** Red spruce, wildflowers, whitetail deer, and songbirds suffered. North Carolina banned deer hunting for five years in **1927**, and both states set up game and wildlife commissions. The loss of the forest also ended traditional subsistence life.

##### Park Advocates
* **Harvey Broome:** A Knoxville lawyer, Smoky Mountains Hiking Club president, and a founder of the **Wilderness Society**. *[Transcript: "Harvey Broom."]*
* **Horace Kephart:** A writer who saw wilderness as an escape from urban industrial life. *[Transcript: "horse cupboard," "horse kept art."]*
* **Chase Ambler:** An Ohio physician who co-formed the **Appalachian National Park Association** in **1899**. *[Transcript: "chased ambler."]*
* **Anne and Willis P. Davis:** A Knoxville couple who saw how tourism helped businesses near **Yellowstone** and promoted a park at home. They helped create the **Smoky Mountains Conservation Association**.
* **David C. Chapman:** A leader of the park campaign.
* **Ben Morton:** The Knoxville mayor who proposed that the city pay one-third of the purchase price.
* **Rockefeller Funding:** A philanthropic fund covered part of the cost. *[Transcript: "Dolores Felman Rockefeller." Correct name: the Laura Spelman Rockefeller Memorial.]*
* **Walker Sisters:** Allowed to stay through a lease arrangement.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **A long-shaped landscape:** Today's Smokies reflect centuries of Indigenous, settler, and industrial influence, and they are not a perfect emblem of the past.
2. **Development vs. preservation:** The old Southern dilemma was how to develop the economy without destroying the place, so the park was never inevitable.
3. **Tourism as an economic argument:** Civic leaders came to see a park as more profitable long term than logging.
4. **Grassroots success, local cost:** Ordinary donors helped create the park, but thousands of people lost their home places.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture traces how people shaped the Smokies over centuries and how a campaign turned them into a national park.

1. **A Special Place:** The **Smokies** are known for rainfall, mist, and biodiversity across **500,000+ acres**. *[Correction: they lie in Tennessee and North Carolina, not South Carolina.]* People valued the area long before the park existed.
2. **Cherokee and Settler Impact:** The **Cherokee** farmed, burned, and hunted, and adapted European plants and practices. *[Correction: earlier Native peoples preceded them.]* **Settlers** feared the forest, and bounties reduced predators. Without predators, squirrels became such a nuisance that the state imposed a scalp tax. The **passenger pigeon** went extinct, and native cane brakes were destroyed for hog and cattle raising.
3. **The Lumber Era (Late 1800s–Early 1900s):** Those effects were minor compared with corporate **logging**. Companies like **Champion** bought thousands of acres and clearcut **60%** of the Smokies in 20 years. Plants, deer, and songbirds suffered, some changes seemed irreversible, and mountain subsistence culture faded. Game laws and public concern followed.
4. **Tourists and Advocates:** Railroads and automobiles brought visitors seeking relief from modern life. **Broome**, **Kephart**, and **Ambler** became advocates, and Ambler's **Appalachian National Park Association** formed in **1899**.
5. **The Park Campaign (1920s–1940):** After visiting **Yellowstone**, **Anne and Willis Davis** promoted a park in Knoxville. A New York publicity firm may have added "Great" to the name. The hard part was getting the land from timber interests and about **15,000** residents in eastern Tennessee and western Tennessee. *[Correction: East Tennessee and western North Carolina.]* Mayor **Ben Morton**, schoolchildren's pennies, the states, the **Rockefeller** fund *[correct name: Laura Spelman Rockefeller Memorial]*, and federal money in the 1930s made it possible. **Franklin Roosevelt** dedicated the park in **September 1940**. Except for a few like the **Walker Sisters**, most residents left, and compensation could not replace a "home place."

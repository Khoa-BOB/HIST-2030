### Lecture 35: The Backlash to Reconstruction & the Early History of the Ku Klux Klan in Tennessee — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### The Early Ku Klux Klan
* **Three Phases of Klan Activity:** Widespread Klan activity came in three waves: the **1860s–1870s**, a revival in the **1910s–1920s**, and again in the **1950s–1960s**.
* **Founding in Pulaski (1866):** The Klan formed in **Pulaski, Tennessee**, in **1866**. Its founders were **Frank McCord, Richard Reed, John Lester, Calvin Jones, John Booker Kennedy, and James Crowe**: men in their mid-20s to mid-30s from comfortable backgrounds who saw themselves as "gentlemen of education and fine taste."
* **Elaine Frantz Parsons:** Historian and author of *Ku-Klux: The Birth of the Klan during Reconstruction*. She argues the first Klan probably was **not founded for racial conflict**: it began as a secret society of restless, bored young ex-Confederates looking for purpose. But organized white violence soon seemed "attractive and pragmatic" to Southern partisans, and the Klan gave them a network for it.
* **"Social Club" Apologia:** Defenders describe the Klan's origins as an innocent social club. The lecturer says this is not entirely false but ignores how fast the group **mutated** into violence.
* **Shift to Paramilitary Terrorism (1867–1871):** Violence began sporadically around Pulaski in 1866–1867, then grew dramatically from **spring 1868 to mid-1871**. In that period the Klan became a regional and national movement tied to the **Democratic Party** and former Confederates, a form of **domestic terrorism**.
* **Targets & Assassinations:** The Klan targeted **Black people** and **white Republicans** who supported Black civil and political rights. Its violence included political assassinations, such as that of a **state senator in 1867**. Similar groups elsewhere included the **White League**.
* **Costume & Ritual:** Elaborate costumes borrowed from **carnivals and minstrel shows**, with an almost clown-like quality, were combined with rituals meant to look supernatural, like **ghosts of the Confederacy**. The ritual structure made the terror feel controlled and meaningful to members.
* **Disempowerment & Manhood:** Parsons calls Klan performance partly an expression of white Southern men's sense of **failure as patriarchs** after defeat. Many had grounded their manhood in the power to inflict violence on enslaved people, and defeat, disability, and loss left them angry and powerless. The point is to explain the Klan's appeal, not to excuse it.
* **Nathan Bedford Forrest:** Former Confederate general who became the Klan's **Grand Wizard in 1867**.

##### State & Federal Response
* **Anti-Klan Law (1868):** Tennessee passed a severe law with **fines and imprisonment** for any connection to the Klan. The governor was also authorized to raise **State Guard** (militia) regiments against it.
* **Federal Anti-Klan Legislation:** Congress passed several bills against Southern paramilitary activity, and federal prosecutions **largely broke organized Klan activity by the early 1870s**. Even so, Klan members and their allies would soon return to power.

##### Redemption in Tennessee (1869–1870)
* **DeWitt Clinton Senter:** Took over as governor when **Brownlow went to the U.S. Senate**. In the **1869 election** against **William B. Stokes** (both claiming to be Radical Republicans), Senter courted conservatives and used his office to **loosen voter restrictions**, letting tens of thousands of **ex-Confederates** vote.
* **Conservative/Democratic Landslide (1869):** The newly enfranchised voters shifted the legislature to men who would soon call themselves **Democrats**. They **repealed the anti-Klan act** and the **State Guard act** and called a new constitutional convention.
* **Constitutional Convention (January 1870):** Had few Radicals and **no African American delegates**. It was chaired by **John C. Brown**, a former Confederate general and Klan member, who was then **elected governor in 1870**.
* **Constitution of 1870:** Established **universal male suffrage**, so former Confederates could no longer be disfranchised, and added a **poll tax** meant to limit Black voting.
* **Post-1870 Party Geography:** Democrats held **Middle and West Tennessee**, while Republicans kept a presence in **East Tennessee**.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **From "social club" to terror network:** The Klan's possibly non-political origins mattered little. Within two years it became a paramilitary organization serving the Democratic Party and ex-Confederates.
2. **Defeat and disfranchisement as fuel:** Barred from voting, defeated, and stripped of mastery over enslaved people, many white Southern men turned to the Klan as a way to regain political, social, and racial control outside the formal system.
3. **The Klan lost the battle but its cause won:** State and federal crackdowns broke the organized Klan, but Senter's franchise loosening let ex-Confederates retake Tennessee within a year.
4. **Redemption through the constitution:** The 1870 Constitution ended Confederate disfranchisement and added a poll tax, using seemingly race-neutral voting rules (as warned in Lecture 32) to start rolling back Black political power.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture covers the white backlash to Radical Reconstruction, focusing on the Ku Klux Klan's origins in Tennessee and the political "redemption" that returned ex-Confederates to power by 1870.

1. **The Klan's Origins in Pulaski (1866–1867):** Widespread Klan activity came in three waves (**1860s–70s**, **1910s–20s**, **1950s–60s**). The first began in **Pulaski, Tennessee**, in **1866**, founded by six educated, comfortable young ex-Confederates. Historian **Elaine Frantz Parsons** concludes the Klan probably was not founded for racial conflict. Its founders were restless and bored and looking for purpose, and they built a secret society full of ritual. Apologists call it a "social club," and the lecturer says that is not entirely wrong but misses how quickly it changed.
2. **Becoming a Terrorist Organization (1867–1871):** For disfranchised, defeated ex-Confederates, the Klan offered a network for changing the South by force when they could not change it at the ballot box. Violence began sporadically around Pulaski and **exploded from spring 1868 to mid-1871**, as copycat groups tied to the **Democratic Party** spread across the South. The Klan targeted **Black people** and **white Republicans**, including through political assassinations, and **Nathan Bedford Forrest** became Grand Wizard in **1867**. The lecturer calls this **domestic terrorism**, a description of its methods and goals rather than an exaggeration.
3. **Ritual, Costume & Masculinity:** Klan costumes borrowed from **carnivals and minstrel shows** and were paired with rituals meant to make members look like **ghosts of the Confederacy**. Parsons reads this performance as an expression of white men's **disempowerment** and failure as patriarchs after the war, especially for men who had grounded their manhood in violence against enslaved people. Understanding this explains the Klan's appeal without excusing it.
4. **The Crackdown (1868–early 1870s):** Tennessee passed a harsh **anti-Klan law in 1868**, with fines and imprisonment, and authorized **State Guard** militia regiments. Federal legislation and prosecutions largely **crushed organized Klan activity by the early 1870s**. Even so, the people behind the Klan soon returned to power.
5. **Redemption in Tennessee (1869–1870):** When **Brownlow** left for the U.S. Senate, **DeWitt Clinton Senter** became governor. To beat fellow Radical **William B. Stokes** in **1869**, Senter loosened voting restrictions, and tens of thousands of ex-Confederates voted, producing a **conservative/Democratic landslide**. The new legislature **repealed the anti-Klan and State Guard acts** and called a **constitutional convention (January 1870)** with no Black delegates. It was chaired by **John C. Brown**, an ex-Confederate general and Klan member who became **governor in 1870**. The **Constitution of 1870** established universal male suffrage, ending Confederate disfranchisement, and added a **poll tax** to limit Black voting. Democrats now dominated **Middle and West Tennessee**, while Republicans held on in **East Tennessee**.

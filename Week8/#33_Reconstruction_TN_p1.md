## Summary

### Lecture 33: Reconstruction in Tennessee, Part 1 — The Union Convention of 1865 — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### The Union Convention of January 1865
* **Union Convention (Nashville, January 1865):** Held a few months before the war ended. Nearly all attendees were **Unionists**, many of them **federal soldiers**, and most were "radicals" who at least wanted slavery formally ended. Secessionists were not invited.
* **State Constitutional Amendment Abolishing Slavery:** The convention's main act. It formally ended slavery in Tennessee at about the same time the **13th Amendment** was moving forward nationally.
* **Repudiation of Secession:** Resolutions rejecting Tennessee's "declaration of independence" (secession) and **nullifying all acts of the state legislature after May 1861**, meaning the secessionist government's acts.
* **Voting Restriction:** Anyone not qualified to vote in the **federal election of 1864**, in effect former secessionists, was barred from voting in the new elections for **governor** and **legislators**.

##### Black Tennesseans' Hopes
* **Petition of the Black Residents of Nashville (January 1865):** Addressed to the convention. It claims freedom as a "natural right" and asks delegates to "cut up by the roots the system of slavery." It declares "we know the burdens of citizenship and are ready to bear them," points to **Black service in the Union army** and loyalty to the Union party, and argues that a government of the people should make every man feel invested "without regard to the color of his skin."
* **Constitution of 1834 (implemented 1835):** The petition points out that **free men of color could vote** in Tennessee until this revision took the vote away, so Black suffrage had a historical precedent in the state.

##### Path to Readmission
* **William G. Brownlow:** Recommended by the convention as the **sole candidate for governor**. He becomes the central Radical Republican figure of Tennessee's Reconstruction.
* **Ratification of the 13th Amendment (April 1865):** Carried out by the new legislature.
* **Readmission (1866):** The convention effectively took Tennessee out of the Confederacy, but official readmission to the Union came only in **1866**.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Unionist control of the restart:** By excluding secessionists and restricting the vote, the convention put Reconstruction in Tennessee in the hands of Unionists and Radicals from the start.
2. **Abolition before war's end:** Tennessee ended slavery in its own constitution in early 1865, alongside the national 13th Amendment.
3. **Black Tennesseans claimed citizenship themselves:** The Nashville petition shows freed people using military service, loyalty, and the pre-1835 precedent of Black voting to argue for full citizenship.
4. **Exit first, readmission later:** The convention effectively removed Tennessee from the Confederacy, but formal readmission waited until 1866.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture covers how Tennessee began Reconstruction before the Civil War was even over, through the Union Convention of January 1865, and what Black Tennesseans hoped that moment would bring.

1. **The Union Convention (January 1865):** A convention met in **Nashville** in **January 1865**. It was made up almost entirely of **Unionists**, many of them **federal soldiers**, and most were "radicals" committed at least to ending slavery. Secessionists were not invited.
2. **The Convention's Actions:** Delegates approved a **state constitutional amendment abolishing slavery**, issued resolutions **repudiating secession**, and **nullified the legislature's acts after May 1861**. They called elections for a governor and legislature and barred anyone ineligible to vote in the **1864 federal election**, which in practice excluded former secessionists.
3. **The Petition of Nashville's Black Residents:** Black Nashvillians petitioned the convention, claiming freedom as a **natural right** and urging delegates to uproot slavery completely. They declared themselves ready to bear "the burdens of citizenship," pointed to their **Union army service** and loyalty, and noted that **free men of color had voted** in Tennessee until the **1834 constitution** (in effect 1835). They argued that a true government of the people should include every man regardless of skin color. The lecturer presents the petition as a window into the **hopes of Reconstruction** for freed people.
4. **Toward Readmission (1865–1866):** The convention put forward **William G. Brownlow** as the sole candidate for governor. The new legislature ratified the **13th Amendment** in **April 1865**. Tennessee had effectively left the Confederacy, but formal readmission did not come until **1866**, and the politics of the following years would decide what Reconstruction meant.

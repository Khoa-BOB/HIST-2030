### Lecture 34: Reconstruction, Part 2 — Andrew Johnson, the Memphis Massacre & Civil and Political Rights for Freed People in Tennessee — Entities, Relationships & Summary

#### 1. Key Keyword Entities in Tennessee History

##### Presidential vs. Congressional Reconstruction
* **Andrew Johnson:** A Tennessean who became president after **Lincoln's assassination (April 1865)**. He believed he was carrying out Lincoln's wishes, and despite saying "treason must be made odious," he was **lenient** toward former Confederates, who used that leniency to take back their old positions. He supported abolition but **not racial equality** or expanded rights for Black Americans.
* **Presidential Veto & Impeachment:** Johnson repeatedly **vetoed** congressional civil rights bills. Congress overrode the vetoes, and the **House impeached** him, but the **Senate** fell short of the votes to remove him. By the end of his term he had little practical power.
* **Radical Republicans:** The congressional faction most committed to extending civil (and later political) rights to freed people. They were Johnson's chief opponents.
* **Civil Rights Act of 1866:** Passed a month before the Memphis Massacre. Following **Eric Foner**, it was the first attempt to give **meaning to emancipation** beyond the end of slavery.
* **Ulysses S. Grant:** Elected president in **1868** and **1872**, giving congressional Republicans an ally in the White House.
* **Military Reconstruction Acts:** Divided the rest of the former Confederacy into **military districts**, with ratification of the **14th Amendment** required for readmission. They **did not apply to Tennessee**, which was already readmitted, so Tennessee had **no standing Union army presence** to protect Reconstruction.

##### Racial Violence
* **Memphis Massacre (April 30 – May 3, 1866):** As described by historians **Susan O'Donovan** and **Beverly Bond**, verbal sparring between **white city police** and recently mustered-out **Black Union soldiers** grew into three days of mob violence in **South Memphis**. At least **46 African Americans** (men, women, and children) and **2 white men** were killed. A similar riot in **New Orleans** that year showed that Johnson's approach was failing and that stronger federal protections were needed.

##### Brownlow's Radical Tennessee
* **Governor William G. Brownlow:** A Radical Republican who limited political participation to **loyal Unionists** and worked to **disfranchise Confederates**. He also had a political motive, since ex-Confederates would never vote for him.
* **Franchise Law of June 1865:** Disfranchised anyone who joined the rebellion for **5 to 15 years**. **County clerks** kept voter registries and issued **certificates**, and no one could vote without one. A **stricter 1866 law** created a committee to decide voter eligibility based on Confederate support.
* **Unionist Base:** Tennessee had many white Unionists and Union veterans who stayed in the state after the war. That base let Radicals take power in a way that was not possible in most Southern states.
* **Civil Rights Law (January 1866):** Allowed freedmen to **testify in court**, **make contracts**, **sue and be sued**, and **inherit property**.
* **14th Amendment Ratification:** Tennessee ratified it early, amid controversy, which led to **readmission in 1866**.
* **Black Male Suffrage (1867):** Granted by the **state legislature**, ahead of the 15th Amendment. Bars on Black **officeholding** were removed by **1868**. Brownlow was **re-elected in 1867** largely with Black votes, one of the first major elections in which African American voters shaped the result.
* **15th Amendment & Tennessee:** Opponents argued it was unnecessary because Black men in Tennessee could already vote. Tennessee **refused to ratify** it and only did so belatedly in the late 20th century, though the amendment still applied to the state.

##### Freedpeople Seizing Opportunity
* **Black Economic Independence:** By mid-1866, **Pulaski** had eight Black-owned businesses (four stores, three restaurants, a saloon) plus Black carpenters, blacksmiths, wagon makers, and brick layers. **William F. Yardley** of Knoxville became an agent for New York Life Insurance. Knoxville had a Black doctor by 1870, and Memphis admitted a Black lawyer to the bar in 1868.
* **Louis Winters & Henry Harding:** **Winters** came to Nashville in 1865 with $40 in goods and was worth about **$3,500** within five years as a grocer. Nashville hotel keeper **Henry Harding**'s fortune reached about **$35,000** by 1870.
* **Fisk Free School (1866):** Later **Fisk University**. Schools and **churches** became key institutions for Black Tennesseans.
* **Freedmen's Bureau:** A federal agency that helped freed people negotiate labor contracts, find work, and get other assistance.
* **Black Local Officeholders:** No Black man held **state** office in Tennessee during Reconstruction, unlike in states such as South Carolina. Black men did win **local** office: **W. S. McTeer**, a Maryville merchant, was elected to the **board of aldermen (1868)**, **Edward Shaw** of Memphis was elected to the **Shelby County Commission (1869)**, and Black men held municipal posts in Nashville.

##### Key Dynamics & Evolutionary Drivers
1. **Johnson's leniency shifted power to Congress:** Johnson's tolerance of ex-Confederates and his vetoes, together with violence like the **Memphis Massacre**, convinced Congress that Reconstruction needed real enforcement. Congress responded with the **Civil Rights Act**, the **14th Amendment**, and military reconstruction.
2. **Early readmission cut both ways:** Rejoining in 1866 spared Tennessee military reconstruction but also left it **without federal troops** to protect freed people and Republican government.
3. **Radical rule rested on disfranchisement:** A strong Unionist base plus laws keeping Confederates from voting let Brownlow's Radicals expand Black civil rights and suffrage. That same disfranchisement built up resentment.
4. **Freedom put into practice:** Black Tennesseans voted, held local office, built businesses, schools, and churches, and used the Freedmen's Bureau. This opportunity provoked the backlash that followed.

#### 3. Lecture Summary

This lecture follows Tennessee's Reconstruction from Andrew Johnson's presidency and the Memphis Massacre through the Radical Republican policies of Governor Brownlow, and looks at how freed people used the opportunities of the postwar moment.

1. **Andrew Johnson's Presidency (1865–1869):** After **Lincoln's assassination** in **April 1865**, Tennessean **Andrew Johnson** became president. He thought he was following Lincoln's wishes, but he was **lenient** toward former Confederates despite his harsh rhetoric, and they quickly moved to reclaim power. He supported abolition but opposed racial equality and used his **veto** against congressional civil rights measures. Congress overrode his vetoes, and the House **impeached** him, though the Senate failed to remove him. By the end of his term he was largely powerless, and **Ulysses S. Grant** (elected **1868** and **1872**) gave Republicans an ally in the presidency.
2. **The Memphis Massacre & the Turn to Congressional Reconstruction (1866):** From **April 30 to May 3, 1866**, a confrontation between white police and Black Union veterans became a three-day white rampage through **South Memphis**. At least **46 African Americans** and **2 white men** were killed, and **New Orleans** saw similar violence. These events showed that Johnson's approach was failing. Congress had already passed the **Civil Rights Act of 1866**, which Foner calls the first attempt to give meaning to emancipation, and it then turned to the **14th Amendment** and the **Military Reconstruction Acts**. Because Tennessee had already been **readmitted in 1866**, those acts did not apply there, and the state had **no standing Union army** to enforce Reconstruction.
3. **Brownlow's Radical Republican Tennessee (1865–1868):** Backed by a large Unionist and Union-veteran population, **Governor William G. Brownlow** limited voting to loyal men. The **June 1865** law disfranchised rebels for **5–15 years** under a certificate system, and an **1866** law tightened it. These laws were justified as keeping traitors out of government, but they also kept Brownlow's opponents from the polls and fed resentment. The Radicals passed a **January 1866** law letting freedmen testify, make contracts, sue, and inherit, ratified the **14th Amendment** early, and in **1867** gave **Black men the vote** through the legislature, removing officeholding bars by **1868**. Black voters helped re-elect Brownlow in **1867**. Tennessee **rejected the 15th Amendment**, claiming it was unnecessary, and did not ratify it until the late 20th century.
4. **Freedpeople Seize the Moment (1865–1870):** Freed people built economic independence: Black-owned businesses in **Pulaski**, **William F. Yardley** in Knoxville insurance, grocer **Louis Winters** and hotel keeper **Henry Harding** in Nashville. They founded institutions like the **Fisk Free School (1866)** and churches, used the **Freedmen's Bureau**, and moved to cities like Nashville and Memphis. No Black man held state office, but men such as **W. S. McTeer** (Maryville aldermen, 1868) and **Edward Shaw** (Shelby County Commission, 1869) won local office.
5. **A Contested Opportunity:** The postwar years were a real **moment of opportunity** for Black Tennesseans, but they were fiercely contested. Former Confederates and white Southern Democrats wanted power back, and their backlash took shape in paramilitary groups, above all the **Ku Klux Klan**, which began in Tennessee and is the subject of the next lecture.

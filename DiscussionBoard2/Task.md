## Discussion Board Response

Across Archives 1–3, I searched ProQuest Historical Newspapers using “slave trade,” “slave,” “secession,” and “civil war.” I wanted to understand how Tennessee newspapers discussed slavery, disunion, and the war’s aftermath. These searches showed how national conflicts reached Tennessee readers through local reporting and reprinted articles.

In Archive 1, the *Nashville Whig*’s [“Laws of the U. States”](https://www.proquest.com/hnpnashvilletennesseanshell/docview/918346430?accountid=14582) reproduced the federal act approved March 3, 1819, authorizing officials to “seize, take, and bring into any port of the United States” American vessels involved in the prohibited trade. “Southern Slave-holding; and Northern Slave-importing” criticized Northern merchants’ involvement in that trade. Together, these sources reveal a distinction between opposing the Atlantic slave trade and opposing slavery itself.

In Archive 2, my “secession” search located two articles in Nashville’s *Republican Banner*. “Position of Tennessee in 1832–3” (June 5, 1851, p. 2) recalled Union meetings in Henry and Hawkins Counties during the Nullification Crisis. One resolution pledged to preserve the Union “peaceably if practicable.” “Secession Meeting in Winchester, Tennessee” (December 1, 1860, p. 2) reported that P. Turney and M. Turney demanded immediate secession, while A. S. Colyar opposed hasty action and favored a state convention. Together, these articles show how county meetings connected communities to national politics and reveal competing views of Union, slavery, and state authority.

Archive 3 revealed that fears of conflict persisted after the fighting ended. [“Civil War: Steps Taken by the Radical Leaders for the Conflict,” *Republican Banner*, November 1, 1866, p. 1](https://www.proquest.com/hnpnashvilletennesseanshell/docview/939259160?accountid=14582), warned of “a war of political parties and of neighborhoods.” “Civil War Threatened” (October 2, 1867) similarly portrayed the struggle between Andrew Johnson and Congress as potentially violent. These were partisan warnings, rather than proof that another war was inevitable.

The secession articles offered little about everyday economic life, and the collected sources left enslaved people’s own experiences largely unheard. Personal narratives, letters, court records, and Freedmen’s Bureau records could provide perspectives missing from these newspaper accounts.

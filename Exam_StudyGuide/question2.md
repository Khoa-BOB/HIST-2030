## Introduction

Long before Tennessee was a state, a territory, or even a colonial frontier, it was a Native homeland. People first arrived about 13,000 years ago, and the Western Valley "may have been the most densely settled region during the [Paleo-Indian] period in the US" **[Week 1 – #2 The first Tennesseans]**. For the next three centuries after European contact, Native peoples were never just background to Tennessee's history; they were central actors in it. Disease, trade, and slaving shattered the Mississippian world, and the survivors re-formed into powerful nations such as the Cherokee and Chickasaw. These nations were first courted as allies by European empires, then treated as obstacles by land-hungry settlers. Treaties that began as agreements between trading partners and military allies turned into a steady chain of land cessions, each one usually legalizing settlement that had already happened. By the 1830s, that process, driven by settlers' demand for Native land and backed by state and federal power, ended in the forced removal of most Native people from Tennessee. Understanding this history is essential, because the state itself was built on the land those treaties took.

## The First Tennesseans and the Shatter Zone

The "First Tennesseans" were the Native peoples who lived in the region for thousands of years before Europeans arrived. Their history runs through four broad stages. **Paleo-Indian** (Clovis) hunters arrived about 13,000–12,000 years ago, and a mastodon kill site in Middle Tennessee dates back more than 13,000 years. **Archaic** peoples moved with the seasons. **Woodland** peoples built permanent villages, grew corn and squash, and raised burial mounds such as **Pinson Mounds**. By around 900 CE, **Mississippian** societies had large towns ruled by a hierarchy of chiefs and priests **[Week 1 – #2 The first Tennesseans]**. Europeans first arrived with **Hernando de Soto**, who entered East Tennessee in 1540 **[Week 1 – #2 The first Tennesseans]**. The consequences were catastrophic but gradual. Historian **Robbie Ethridge** calls the years 1540–1715 the **"Mississippian Shatter Zone."** Native population fell by "around 90%" over about two centuries because of warfare and Old World diseases, including a 1696 smallpox outbreak **[Week 1 – #3 Firt Tennesseans, pt2]**. Just as important was "the incorporation of indigenous people into a modern world economy" dominated by Europeans, built on deerskins, beaver pelts, and guns **[Week 1 – #3 Firt Tennesseans, pt2]**. The Indian slave trade caught Native groups in "a cycle... of slaving, trading and weapons escalation": guns had to be paid for with skins or captives, and captives were taken in raids. Even the Cherokee, who had criticized slaving in the late 1600s, "armed their own slave [raiders]" around 1700 **[Week 1 – #3 Firt Tennesseans, pt2]**. The small Mississippian chiefdoms collapsed, and survivors **coalesced** into the large historic nations: the **Cherokee, Chickasaw, Creek, and Choctaw**. The **Yamasee War of 1715**, a Native uprising against the slave trade, marked what Ethridge calls "the emergence of the colonial south" **[Week 1 – #3 Firt Tennesseans, pt2]**.

## Empires, Alliance, and the Anglo-Cherokee War

Early contact between Native peoples and Europeans was not simply conquest. For much of the 1700s, the Cherokee held real bargaining power. In the French and Indian War (1754–1763), both Britain and France needed Native allies, and the Cherokee used that leverage: in effect, "we'll fight alongside you but we need a little something [in] return" **[Week 2 – European empires and Tennesee History]**. What they asked for was a fort "to protect Cherokee women and children," and in 1756–57 the British built **Fort Loudoun** in the Overhill country near **Chota** **[Week 2 – European empires and Tennesee History]**. The alliance fell apart amid frontier killings. South Carolina's governor **Lyttelton** cut off arms shipments, and **Oconostota's** peace delegation was taken hostage at Fort Prince George, where the hostages were later killed. The result was the **Anglo-Cherokee War**: Fort Loudoun was besieged and surrendered in 1760, and a British expedition burned Cherokee towns and crops in 1761 **[Week 2 – European empires and Tennesee History]**. Historian **Daniel Tortora** sums up the cost: "from 1758 to 1761 at least 1/3 of the Cherokee had died from combat and disease" **[Week 2 – European empires and Tennesee History]**. The Cherokee had entered the imperial contest as a valued ally and came out of it badly weakened.

## Sources of Conflict with Settlers: Land, Deer, and War

As British settlers crossed the mountains in the 1760s and 1770s, the main sources of conflict were **competition over resources**, above all **land**. The first white men to arrive in large numbers, the **long hunters** such as Daniel Boone, came for deer. Historian **Drew Swanson** notes that whitetail deer were "among the most important natural resources for southeastern Native Americans," supplying food and hides and even holding a place in Cherokee religion through the **Little Deer** spirit **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt. 1]**. As the herds shrank, Native people came "into more conflict with these settlers because... everyone is extracting a variety of things from this mountain region" **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt. 1]**. Land was the deeper problem. The **Proclamation of 1763** banned settlement west of the Appalachians, but settlers ignored it, and after the boundary treaties of 1768 and 1770 "settlers will either misunderstand the lines or disregard them" **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt. 1; The Frontier, pt 2]**. The American Revolution turned this friction into open war. In 1776 British agents backed the Cherokee, who launched a three-pronged offensive against the settlements. In the aftermath, "the Cherokees' hold on the region is going to be greatly disrupted," which "energize[d] settlers to move into the Tennessee region" **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt 3]**. Conflict continued after the war: the Cumberland settlers of 1779–80 lived in "survival mode" **[Week 3 – The Frontier, pt 5]**, and supporters of the State of Franklin wanted statehood partly because of "hostilities and conflict with indigenous groups" **[Week 3 – The Frontier, pt 6]**.

## The Changing Nature of Treaties

Treaties between Native nations and newcomers changed in who negotiated them, what they were for, and how much power the Native side held. In the 1750s, agreements were **alliances** between the Cherokee and an empire, exchanging military help for protection and trade **[Week 2 – European empires and Tennesee History]**. By the 1760s–1770s they had become **boundary lines** that settlers routinely crossed, and then deals made directly by settlers and speculators. Around 1772 the **Watauga** settlers leased land straight from the Cherokee, "a real backwards tenancy" that gave them a claim that "might lead to possession" **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt 2]**. In March 1775, judge **Richard Henderson**, acting "without sanction from either British or colonial authorities," made the **Treaty of Sycamore Shoals** (the Transylvania Purchase), which opened Middle Tennessee and split Cherokee opinion, with **Dragging Canoe** opposing it **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt 3]**. After the 1776 war, the 1777 peace treaties with Virginia and North Carolina brought "the formal [cession] of land with new boundaries legalizing all four settlements" **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt 3]**. The pattern was now clear: settlers crossed the line, violence followed, and a new treaty made the squatting legal. The **Treaty of Holston** (1791), negotiated by territorial governor **William Blount**, legalized settlement south of the French Broad River, where about 3,600 people were already living illegally **[Week 3 – Southwest Territory]**. After statehood, cessions got larger. In the **1818 Chickasaw Cession**, **Andrew Jackson** and **Isaac Shelby** used bribery, among other tactics, to acquire all of West Tennessee for $300,000, "the largest single [cession] of land" in the state's history **[Week 3 – Early statehood]**. Over about seventy years, treaties went from agreements between allies to instruments of dispossession, and Native bargaining power disappeared with them.

## Adaptation, Resistance, and Removal

Native peoples responded to these pressures by both resisting and adapting. After the **New Madrid earthquakes** of 1811–12, historian **Conevery Bolton Valencius** writes that "many Indians interpreted the quake as the [calamitous] anger of the great spirit at the loss of Indian culture and Indian lands," which fed Tecumseh's confederation, a Cherokee religious revival, and conflict among the Creeks **[Week 4 – NewMaridEarthquaks+SecondGreatAwakening in TN]**. By the 1820s, repeated cessions had left the Cherokee "largely confined to one small corner... in southeastern Tennessee" **[Week 4 – Antebellum_PoliticsAndEcon_pt2]**. There they built a republican-style government, some members adopted Christianity, private property, and slaveholding, **Sequoyah** created a syllabary, and the nation published the bilingual **Cherokee Phoenix** **[Week 4 – Antebellum_PoliticsAndEcon_pt2]**. Adaptation did not protect them, because the real issue was that "people wanted a very valuable land that [the] Cherokee occupied, and so that's at the center" **[Week 4 – Antebellum_PoliticsAndEcon_pt2]**. **Andrew Jackson**, who had helped "orchestrate the land [cession] and essentially defeat of indigenous people in the southeast," signed the **Indian Removal Act of 1830** as president. Cherokee sovereignty was "tested over legal battles," while Georgia and Alabama used "every single mechanism possible" to force removal **[Week 4 – Antebellum_PoliticsAndEcon_pt2]**. The Cherokee were the last to sign a removal treaty, concluded in **December 1835**. Of about **16,000 Cherokees** forced from their homeland, "potentially as many [as] 4000 people" died, many of them passing through Memphis on the way west, and Chattanooga was founded on former Cherokee land, "the end of an era" **[Week 4 – Antebellum_PoliticsAndEcon_pt2]**.

## Conclusion

The history of Native peoples is essential to understanding Tennessee, for three reasons. First, it makes up most of the region's human past: about 13,000 years of it came before statehood **[Week 1 – #2 The first Tennesseans]**. Second, Native nations shaped every major turning point, from the imperial contest that produced Fort Loudoun to the frontier conflicts that drove the Franklin movement, Blount's territorial government, and Andrew Jackson's rise **[Week 2 – European empires and Tennesee History; Week 3 – The Frontier, pt 6, Southwest Territory; Week 4 – Antebellum_PoliticsAndEcon_pt2]**. Third, the state was literally built on Native land. Watauga, Sycamore Shoals, Holston, and the 1818 Chickasaw Cession each opened a new part of Tennessee to settlement **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt 2, pt 3; Week 3 – Southwest Territory, Early statehood]**. The story runs from the First Tennesseans through the Shatter Zone, from alliance to cession, and finally to removal. Along the way, Native peoples were not just victims but actors who coalesced, negotiated, fought, and adapted. Their forced exodus in the 1830s is the defining consequence of Tennessee's founding, and any account of the state's history that leaves them out tells only part of the story.

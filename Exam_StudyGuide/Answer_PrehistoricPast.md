# Short Answer: Tennessee's Prehistoric Past

> **Question:** Generally, historians study human history through written evidence, but Tennessee's prehistory is worth considering too, if only to better understand the vast environmental and geologic changes that have transformed the state over time. How might you characterize Tennessee's prehistoric past? What non-human species inhabited the area, what major environmental changes occurred in the deep past, and why are we still learning about the state's prehistoric past?

---

Tennessee's prehistoric past is best described as a long story of environmental change, in which the land moved from sea to swamp to forest long before any written records existed. For historians, prehistory is the period "before we have written records," and since "written documents are at the core of historical analysis," this era has to be reconstructed from material evidence instead (Lecture 1, "Tennessee History & Deep Time"). Even so, the lecture argues that "human history and material history are not all that separate," because the geology of the deep past shaped the land people later lived on (Lecture 1). The land that became Tennessee began forming about **1 billion years ago** while it was still underwater, and the metamorphic rocks exposed in the **Blue Ridge Mountains** date from that time (Lecture 1). In the **Paleozoic Era**, a shallow sea covered the state and was "home to a variety of marine creatures" whose fossils have been preserved. Mountain building created swampy deltas, and their plant material eventually formed Tennessee's **coal deposits**. The state then rose "above sea level, mostly by the end of the Paleozoic era" (Lecture 1). During the **Mesozoic Era**, only **West Tennessee** stayed under shallow seawater, with "clams, oysters, and snails," while the rest of the state held "swamps and forest" where "a variety of prehistoric species of elephants [and] alligators" roamed (Lecture 1). **Mastodon** remains show that even though "the ice sheets did not reach Tennessee during the ice age a cooler climate prevailed" (Lecture 1).

We are still learning about this past because new evidence keeps being found, and the **Gray Fossil Site** in East Tennessee is the lecture's main example. In **2000**, a construction crew "discovered fossils during a road reconstruction project," and the site became a center of scientific research run by East Tennessee State University (Lecture 1). It preserves a "forested pond ecosystem" from about **4 to 5 million years ago** (the early **Pliocene**), when East Tennessee had "a slightly warmer climate," and it contains remains of "over 200 species of animals and plants," including alligators, mastodons, and rhinos (Lecture 1). Some finds are "entirely unique in the Americas, including a red panda," and the most significant discovery was "a new species of extinct rhinoceros" that is "only found in one place in the world"; one of its complete skeletons is nicknamed **"Little Guy"** (Lecture 1). As the lecture concludes, Tennessee history "from its prehistoric to its human history is constantly [evolving] because we're constantly finding new records," so the deep past is still being rewritten much like the recent one (Lecture 1).

** New Madrid Earthquakes**
*Source: `Week4/NewMaridEarthquaks+SecondGreatAwakening in TN.md` (quotes from the transcript, line 4)*

How did Americans understand the great earthquakes?
- **Spiritual/religious signs (settlers):** the shaking and sulfur smell read as hell or divine anger: "terrifying roar of underground thunder that represented the threat of damnation to fearful Christians on the frontier… the devil smells of burning sulfur"; "Is this God Himself, shaking the land, his hellfire…?"
  - Tied to the **Second Great Awakening**: the shaking earth was "a direct parallel to the shaking and the way that… the Holy Spirit kind of register[s] in your body."
- **Prophetic signs (Native peoples):** read through land loss and assimilation pressure. Valencius: "Many Indians interpreted the quake as the calamitous anger of the Great Spirit at the loss of Indian culture and Indian lands." This fed three movements: Tecumseh's confederation, the Cherokee revival, and the Creek conflict. Some Cherokees saw it as "anger from the Great Spirit directed at white settlers."
- **Early science:** people described the quakes "in the language of a kind of jolt" (like the new electric batteries); the quakes inspired "amateur scientists… determined to figure out what causes earthquakes."

Why were they forgotten or dismissed?
- The lecture says only "for a variety of reasons" (the professor defers the details to another class). Check the readings if you need specific causes.
- Americans forgot the quakes "over the course of the 19th century… despite the fact that… we have a ton of evidence of their significance."
- "Scientists in the early 20th century, some of them doubted these earthquakes even happened"; the few researchers were "voices in the wilderness… not a lot of reception to these ideas."

Why did the earthquackes become the topic of renewed scientific interest?
- **1970s:** "scientists at St. Louis University and also at the University of Memphis [CERI]… feel that it's important to study the seismic activity in the New Madrid region."
- **Still active and hard to predict:** it is an *intraplate* zone, "a little bit more difficult to predict," with "more recent seismic activity" under West Tennessee.
- **History helps the science:** CERI has "a historian on staff who studies accounts… from newspaper accounts to personal diaries" to map the impact, because "historical evidence has significance even for understanding this seismic zone today."

Long before Tennessee was a state, a territory, or even a colonial frontier, it was a native homeland. People first arrived around 13000 years ago, and the Western Valley may have been the most densely settled region during the Paleo-Indian period in the US (Video #2 -Week1). For the next three centuries after European contact, Native people were never just background to Tennessee's history; they were central actors in it. Disease, trade, and slavery shattered the Mississippian world, and the survivors reformed into powerful nations such as the Cherokee and Chickasaw. These nations were first courted as allies by European empires, then treated as obstacles by land-hungry settlers. Treaties that began as agreements between trading partners and military allies became a steady chain of land cession, each one usually legalizing settlement that had already occurred. By the 1830s, that process, driven by settlers' demand for Native land and backed by state and federal power, ended in the forced removal of most Native people from Tennessee. Understanding this history is essential because the state itself was built on the land those treaties took.

The "First Tennesseans" were the Native peoples who lived in the region for thousands of years before Europeans arrived. Their history runs through four broad stages. Paleo-Indian Clovis hunters arrived about 12,000–13,000 years ago, and a mastodon kill site in Middle Tennessee dates back more than 13,000 years (Video #2- Week1).  Archaic peoples moved with the seasons. Woodland peoples built permanent villages, grew corn and squash, and raised burial mounds such as Pinson Mounds. By around 900 AD, Mississippian societies had large towns ruled by a hierarchy of chiefs and priests (Video #2-Week 1). Europeans first arrived with Hernando de Soto, who entered East Tennessee in 1540  (Video #2-Week 1). The consequences were catastrophic but gradual. Historian Robbie Ethridge calls the years 1540–1715 the Mississippian Shatter Zone. Native population fell by around 90% over about two centuries because of warfare and Old World diseases, including a 1696 smallpox outbreak (Video #3-Week 1). Just as important was the incorporation of indigenous people into a modern world economy dominated by Europeans, built on deerskins, beaver pelts, and guns (Video #3-Week 1). The Indian slave trade caught Native groups in a cycle of slaving, trading, and weapons escalation where guns had to be paid for with skins or captives, and captives were taken in raids. Even the Cherokee, who had criticized slaving in the late 1600s,  armed their own slave raiders around 1700  (Video #3-Week 1). The Indian polities collapsed and were replaced by these large historic nations, including the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Creek, and Choctaw. The Yamasee War of 1715, a Native uprising against the slave trade, marked what Ethridge called the emergence of the colonial South (Video #3-Week 1).


Early contact between Native peoples and Europeans was not simply conquest. For much of the 1700s, the Cherokee held real bargaining power. In the French and Indian War (1754–1763), both Britain and France needed Native allies, and the Cherokee used that leverage; in effect, they fought alongside, but they needed something in return (Video #4 -Week 2). What they asked for was a fort to protect Cherokee women and children, and in 1756–57 the British built Fort Loudoun in Overhill (Video #4-Week 2). The alliance fell apart amid frontier killings. South Carolina's governor Lyttelton cut off arms shipments, and Oconostota's peace delegation was taken hostage at Fort Prince George, where the hostages were later killed. The result was the Anglo-Cherokee War; Fort Loudoun was besieged and surrendered in 1760, and a British expedition burned Cherokee towns and crops in 1761 (Video #4-Week 2). Historian Daniel Tortora sums up the cost from 1758 to 1761, where at least 1/3 of the Cherokee had died from combat and disease (Video#4- Week 2). The Cherokee had entered the imperial contest as a valued ally and came out of it badly weakened.
As British settlers crossed the mountains in the 1760s and 1770s, the main sources of conflict were competition over resources, above all land. The first white men to arrive in large numbers, the long hunters such as Daniel Boone, came for deer. Historian Drew Swanson notes that whitetail deer were among the most important natural resources for southeastern Native Americans, supplying food and hides and even holding a place in Cherokee religion through the Little Deer spirit (Video #5-Week 2). As the herds shrank, Native people came into more conflict with these settlers because everyone was extracting a variety of things from this mountain region (Video#5-Week 2). Land was the deeper problem. The proclamation of 1763 banned settlement west of the Appalachians, but settlers ignored it, and after the boundary treaties of 1768 and 1770, settlers would either misunderstand the lines or disregard them (Video#5 and #6-Week 2). The American Revolution turned this friction into open war. In 1776, British agents backed the Cherokee, who launched a three-pronged offensive against the settlements. In the aftermath, "the Cherokees' hold on the region is going to be greatly disrupted, which energized settlers to move into the Tennessee region (Video #7-Week 2). Conflict continued after the war: the Cumberland settlers of 1779–80 lived in survival mode (Video #9-Week 3), and supporters of the State of Franklin wanted statehood partly because of hostilities and conflict with indigenous groups (Video #10-Week 3).


Treaties between Native nations and newcomers changed in who negotiated them, what they were for, and how much power the Native side held. In the 1750s, agreements were alliances between the Cherokee and an empire, exchanging military help for protection and trade (Video #4-Week 2). By the 1760s–1770s, they had become boundary lines that settlers routinely crossed, and then deals made directly by settlers and speculators. Around 1772, the Watauga settlers leased land straight from the Cherokee, a real backwards tenancy that gave them a claim that might lead to possession (Video #6-Week 2). In March 1775, Judge Richard Henderson, acting without sanction from either British or colonial authorities, made the Treaty of Sycamore Shoals, which opened Middle Tennessee and split Cherokee opinion, so some Cherokees were going to disagree with this (Video #7-Week 2). After the 1776 war, the 1777 peace treaties with Virginia and North Carolina brought the formal cession of land with new boundaries, legalizing all four settlements (Video #7-Week 2). The pattern was now clear: settlers crossed the line, violence followed, and a new treaty made the squatting legal. The Treaty of Holston, negotiated by territorial governor William Blount, legalized settlement south of the French Broad River, where about 3,600 people were already living illegally (Video #11-Week 3).  After statehood, cessions got larger. In the 1818 Chickasaw Cession, Andrew Jackson and Isaac Shelby used bribery, among other tactics, to acquire all of West Tennessee for $300,000, the largest single cession of land in the state's history (Video #12-Week 3). Over about seventy years, treaties went from agreements between allies to instruments of dispossession, and Native bargaining power disappeared with them.


Native peoples responded to these pressures by both resisting and adapting. After the New Madrid earthquakes between 1811 and 1812, historian Conevery Valencius writes that many Indians interpreted the quake as the calamitous anger of the great spirit at the loss of Indian culture and Indian lands, which fed Tecumseh's confederation, a Cherokee religious revival, and conflict among the Creeks (Video #13-Week 4). By the 1820s, repeated cessions had left the Cherokee largely confined to one small corner in southeastern Tennessee (Video #15-Week 4). There they built a republican style government; some members adopted Christianity, private property, and slaveholding; Sequoyah created a syllabary, and the nation published the bilingual Cherokee Phoenix (Video #15-Week 4). Adaptation did not protect them, because the real issue was that people wanted a very valuable land that the Cherokee occupied, and so that's at the center (Video #15-Week 4). Andrew Jackson, who had helped orchestrate the land cession and essentially defeated Indigenous people in the Southeast, signed the Indian Removal Act of 1830 as president. Cherokee sovereignty was tested over legal battles while Georgia and Alabama used every single mechanism possible to force removal (Video #15-Week 4). The Cherokee were the last to sign a removal treaty, concluded in December 1835. Of about 16,000 Cherokees forced from their homeland, potentially as many as 4000 people died, many of them passing through Memphis on the way west, and Chattanooga was founded on former Cherokee land- the end of an era (Video #15-Week 4).


The history of Native peoples is essential to understanding Tennessee, for three reasons. First, it makes up most of the region's human past: about 13,000 years of it came before statehood (Video #2-Week 1). Secondly, Native nations shaped every major turning point, from the imperial contest that produced Fort Loudoun to the frontier conflicts that drove the Franklin movement, Blount's territorial government, and Andrew Jackson's rise (Video#4-Week 2) (Video #10 and #11-Week 3)(Video #15-Week 4). Thirdly, the state was literally built on Native land. Watauga, Sycamore Shoals, Holston, and the 1818 Chickasaw Cession each opened a new part of Tennessee to settlement (Video #6 and #7-Week 2) (Video #11-Week 3). The story runs from the First Tennesseans through the Shatter Zone, from alliance to cession, and finally to removal. Along the way, Native peoples were not just victims but actors who coalesced, negotiated, fought, and adapted. Their forced migration in the 1830s is the defining consequence of Tennessee's founding, and any account of the state's history that leaves them out tells only part of the story.

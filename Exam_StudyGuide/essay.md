**Essay topic**: The essay question will require students to trace the history of indigenous people (Native Americans) in Tennessee history from the “First Tennesseans” to the 1830s. The essay question will ask students to analyze the consequences and interactions between indigenous people, Europeans, and later settlers, the evolution of treaties related to indigenous people in Tennessee (from pre-statehood to the 1830s), and the historical significance (importance) of indigenous people in Tennessee history.

# Essay Prep: Indigenous People in Tennessee History (First Tennesseans → 1830s)

*Sources: Weeks 1–5 lecture notes only. Citation format: **[Week # – lecture file]**. Week 5 (#17–#21) has no material on Native peoples, so the evidence comes from Weeks 1–4.*

## Working thesis

Native peoples shaped Tennessee for about 13,000 years before it became a state, and every stage of its founding happened on their land and through negotiation with them. After European contact, disease, trade, and the slave trade broke up the Mississippian world. The large nations that re-formed afterward (above all the Cherokee) became essential allies and trading partners, and later obstacles, for empires and settlers. Treaties began as tools of alliance and trade. Over time they became a steady series of land cessions, each one legalizing settlement that had already happened. That process pushed the Cherokee into one corner of the state by the 1820s and ended with forced removal in the 1830s.

## Part 1: Chronological evidence (consequences and interactions)

### A. The First Tennesseans (~13,000 BP to 1540)
- Paleo-Indians (Clovis) arrived about 13,000–12,000 years ago. Some of the largest Clovis campsites in the U.S. are in Tennessee's Western Valley, and a mastodon kill site in Middle TN is more than 13,000 years old. **[Week 1 – #2 The first Tennesseans]**
- The sequence runs Archaic (~8,000 BP, seasonal movement) → Woodland (permanent villages, corn and squash, burial mounds such as **Pinson Mounds**) → Mississippian (~900 CE: larger towns and a hierarchy of chiefs and priests). **[Week 1 – #2 The first Tennesseans]**
- **Significance:** Tennessee had complex societies long before Europeans arrived. The region "may have been the most densely settled region during the Paleo-Indian period in the US." **[Week 1 – #2 The first Tennesseans]**

### B. Contact and the "Shatter Zone" (1540–1715)
- **Hernando de Soto** entered East Tennessee in 1540, and other Spanish expeditions followed. **[Week 1 – #2 The first Tennesseans]**
- Historian **Robbie Ethridge** calls this era the **"Mississippian Shatter Zone."** Its causes were military losses, Old World diseases, and "incorporation into a modern world economy" (the deer and beaver trade with the French and English). **[Week 1 – #3 Firt Tennesseans, pt2]**
- **Consequences:**
  - About a **90% population decline**, spread gradually over roughly 200 years. **[Week 1 – #3]**
  - The **Indian slave trade** trapped groups in a "cycle of slaving, trading and weapons escalation" (guns were paid for with skins or captives). **[Week 1 – #3]**
  - Small polities disappeared and **coalesced** into large nations: Cherokee, Chickasaw, Creek, and Choctaw. **[Week 1 – #2 & #3]**
- **Cherokee arc:** The Cherokee first avoided the slave trade and criticized it, then armed their own slave raiders around 1700. A smallpox outbreak in **1696** added pressure. **[Week 1 – #3]**
- The **Yamasee War (1715)** was Native pushback against European powers. It effectively ended the Indian slave trade and marked "the emergence of the colonial South." **[Week 1 – #3]**

### C. Imperial rivalry and Anglo-Cherokee relations (1754–1761)
- In the French and Indian War (1754–1763), both France and Britain needed Native allies. The Cherokee used that leverage: "we'll fight alongside you, but we need a little something in return." **[Week 2 – European empires and Tennesee History]**
- The Cherokee asked for a fort to protect their women and children, and **Fort Loudoun** was built in 1756–57 in Overhill territory near **Chota**. **[Week 2 – European empires; The Frontier, pt. 1]**
- The alliance broke down. Frontier killings led Governor **Lyttelton** to cut off arms shipments, and **Oconostota's** peace delegation was taken hostage at Fort Prince George and the hostages were murdered. The result was the **Anglo-Cherokee War**. **[Week 2 – European empires]**
- Fort Loudoun was besieged and surrendered in 1760. The British punitive expedition of 1761 burned Cherokee towns and crops. **[Week 2 – European empires]**
- **Consequence:** Historian **Daniel Tortora**: "from 1758 to 1761 at least 1/3 of the Cherokee had died from combat and disease… Cherokee towns broke apart… long-standing patterns of authority collapsed." **[Week 2 – European empires]**

### D. The frontier and competition over resources (1760s–1770s)
- **Long hunters** such as Daniel Boone entered an existing Cherokee–European trade network. **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt. 1]**
- Historian **Drew Swanson** describes whitetail deer as "among the most important natural resources for southeastern Native Americans." Each Cherokee used about 9–10 deer a year, and deer were part of Cherokee religion (the **Little Deer** spirit). **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt. 1]**
- **Consequence:** Competition shrank the deer herds, and Native people "come into more conflict with these settlers." **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt. 1]**

### E. Revolution-era war (1776–1780)
- British agents reversed their neutrality policy in 1776 and backed the Cherokee against Patriot settlers. The Cherokee launched a **three-pronged offensive in July 1776**. **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt 3]**
- **Consequence:** "The Cherokees' hold on the region is going to be greatly disrupted," which "energize[d] settlers to move into the Tennessee region." **[Week 2 – The Frontier, pt 3]**
- The Cumberland settlers (1779–80) chose a winter departure specifically to avoid Native attacks and were in "survival mode" amid "repeated conflict with indigenous people." **[Week 3 – The Frontier, pt 5]**

### F. Statehood politics built on Native land (1784–1796)
- Franklin's supporters wanted a new state partly because of "hostilities and conflict with indigenous groups." **[Week 3 – The Frontier, pt 6]**
- Governor **Blount's** most urgent problem in the Southwest Territory was settler squatting on Cherokee land and the violence it caused. About 3,600 people were living illegally south of the French Broad River. **[Week 3 – Southwest Territory]**
- Tensions continued with the Lower Cherokee and the Creeks until around 1795. **[Week 3 – Southwest Territory]**

### G. Native responses in the 1810s
- The **New Madrid earthquakes (1811–12)** came at "a turbulent and critical time" for Native communities. Valencius: "Many Indians interpreted the quake as the calamitous anger of the Great Spirit at the loss of Indian culture and Indian lands." **[Week 4 – NewMaridEarthquaks+SecondGreatAwakening in TN]**
- The earthquakes fed three movements: Tecumseh's confederation, a Cherokee prophetic revival, and conflict among the Creeks. All three pushed resistance to "Americanization" and dislocation. **[Week 4 – NewMaridEarthquaks]**

### H. The Cherokee Nation and Indian Removal (1817–1830s)
- By then the Cherokee in Tennessee "were largely confined to one small corner… in southeastern Tennessee" because of land cessions. **[Week 4 – Antebellum_PoliticsAndEcon_pt2]**
- Cherokee adaptation:
  - an **1817 republican-style government** (per the lecture)
  - Christianity among some members
  - private property and slaveholding
  - **Sequoyah's** 86-character syllabary
  - the bilingual **Cherokee Phoenix** newspaper
  
  **[Week 4 – pt2]**
- Adaptation did not save them: "people wanted a very valuable land that Cherokee occupied, and so that's at the center." **[Week 4 – pt2]**
- **Andrew Jackson** helped "orchestrate the land cession and essentially defeat of indigenous people in the southeast." He also adopted a Creek boy whose parents had been killed by his soldiers. **[Week 4 – pt2]**
- **Indian Removal Act of 1830.** Cherokee sovereignty was "tested over legal battles," and Georgia and Alabama used "every single mechanism possible." **[Week 4 – pt2]**
- **Consequence:** The last Cherokee removal treaty was signed in **December 1835**. Of about **16,000** Cherokees forced out, as many as **4,000** died. Many passed through Memphis on the way to Oklahoma, and some hid in the mountains. **Chattanooga** was founded on the site of John Ross's trading post and ferry: "the end of an era." **[Week 4 – pt2]**

## Part 2: How treaties evolved (pre-statehood → 1830s)

| Date | Treaty / agreement | What it did | Who drove it | Cite |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1754–60s | Anglo-Cherokee alliance (Fort Loudoun) | Military help in exchange for protection and trade; the Cherokee negotiated as a power | Cherokee leverage between empires | [Wk 2 – European empires] |
| 1761 | Peace after the Anglo-Cherokee War | Cherokee gave up Fort Loudoun and released captives | British military force | [Wk 2 – European empires] |
| 1763 | Proclamation of 1763 | Banned white settlement west of the Appalachians; ignored | Crown | [Wk 2 – Frontier pt. 1] |
| 1768 / 1770 | Hard Labor / Lochaber | Redrew settlement lines; settlers "misunderstood or disregarded them" | British officials | [Wk 2 – Frontier pt 2] |
| ~1772 | Watauga lease | Settlers leased Cherokee land directly for goods, a "backwards tenancy" | Settlers bypassing officials | [Wk 2 – Frontier pt 2] |
| Mar 1775 | Sycamore Shoals (Henderson) | Huge private purchase with no official sanction; opened Middle TN. The Cherokee were split (Dragging Canoe opposed) | Land speculators | [Wk 2 – Frontier pt 3] |
| 1777 | Post-war peace treaties (VA and NC) | Land cessions that legalized all four squatter settlements | States, after war | [Wk 2 – Frontier pt 3] |
| 1786 | Franklin–Cherokee treaty (Sevier) | Pushed the settlement line down to the Tennessee River | An unrecognized "state" | [Wk 3 – Frontier pt 6] |
| 1791 | Treaty of Holston (Blount) | Legalized illegal settlement south of the French Broad River | Federal territory | [Wk 3 – Southwest Territory] |
| Oct 1798 | Cherokee cession | "More land cessions will follow through a series of treaty negotiations" | State of Tennessee / U.S. | [Wk 3 – Early statehood] |
| 1818 | Chickasaw Cession (Jackson and Shelby) | Gave up all of West TN for $300,000, using bribery. The largest single cession in TN; Shelby and Hardin counties were created at once | Federal negotiators | [Wk 3 – Early statehood] |
| 1830 / Dec 1835 | Indian Removal Act → Cherokee removal treaty | Complete removal from TN; led to the Trail of Tears | Jackson, Georgia, Alabama | [Wk 4 – pt2] |

**The pattern to name in the essay:** treaty → settlers ignore the line → violence → new treaty that legalizes the squatting, as **[Week 2 – Week2.md focus #2]** points out. The treaties changed in three ways:
1. **Who negotiated:** empires → private speculators and settlers → states → the federal government.
2. **Their purpose:** *alliance and trade* → *boundaries* → *cession* → *removal*.
3. **Native bargaining power:** strong in the 1750s, when the Cherokee could play empires against each other; gone by the 1830s.

## Part 3: Historical significance (the "so what")
1. **Tennessee's human history is mostly Native history.** About 13,000 years of it came before statehood. **[Week 1 – #2]**
2. **They shaped the imperial contest.** The Cherokee alliance was valuable enough that Britain built Fort Loudoun, and it helped the British in the French and Indian War. **[Week 2 – European empires]**
3. **Their land made the state.** Every stage of settlement and statehood (Watauga, Sycamore Shoals, Holston, the 1818 Chickasaw Cession) depended on taking Native land. Settlers flooded into West TN "right away" after 1818. **[Week 2 – Frontier pt 2–3; Week 3 – Southwest Territory, Early statehood]**
4. **They drove frontier politics.** Conflict with Native nations was one reason for the Franklin movement, it was Blount's most urgent issue, and it launched Jackson's career. **[Week 3 – Frontier pt 6, Southwest Territory; Week 4 – pt2]**
5. **They adapted and resisted, not only suffered.** Examples: coalescence, Cherokee leverage, the 1776 offensive, the Fort Loudoun siege, the spiritual movements after New Madrid, and the constitution, syllabary, and newspaper. **[Weeks 1–4]**
6. **Their removal is the defining moral legacy of the era.** It is central to judging Jackson, the "complicated figure" who was Tennessee's first president. **[Week 4 – pt2]**

## Suggested essay outline
1. **Intro:** Native peoples as Tennessee's first and longest history, plus the thesis above.
2. **Body 1, deep time to the Shatter Zone:** de Soto, disease, the slave trade, coalescence (Ethridge).
3. **Body 2, empires and alliance:** Fort Loudoun, the Anglo-Cherokee War (Tortora's one-third death toll).
4. **Body 3, the treaty cycle:** Hard Labor/Lochaber → Watauga → Sycamore Shoals → 1777 → Holston → 1818 (use the table).
5. **Body 4, adaptation and removal:** the New Madrid movements, Cherokee institution-building, the 1830 Act, 1835, the Trail of Tears, Chattanooga.
6. **Conclusion:** significance points 3–6. Tennessee was built on Native land through an escalating treaty process.

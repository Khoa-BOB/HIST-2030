# Short Answer: New Madrid Earthquakes

> **Question:** In 1811-1812, the New Madrid earthquakes rocked the region of western Tennessee, western Kentucky, eastern Arkansas, and southeastern Missouri. For some people, the earthquakes were more than geologic occurrences; they held spiritual significance. Why?

---

The New Madrid earthquakes were enormous, disorienting events, so people in the region naturally looked for meaning in them. Beginning in **December 1811** and continuing into **February 1812**, three major quakes of roughly seven or eight on the Richter scale, along with thousands of smaller ones, created sand blows and fissures that seemed to swallow homes and cattle, raised the land into a dome, formed **Reelfoot Lake**, and even made the Mississippi River run backwards for a time (Lecture, "New Madrid Earthquakes & the Second Great Awakening in TN"). For **Native communities**, the quakes came at a turbulent and critical time, as the Cherokee and Creek faced dislocation "through land [cession], treaty, war," and pressure to assimilate (Lecture, "New Madrid Earthquakes"). Quoting historian **Conevery Bolton Valencius**, the lecture explains that Native peoples interpreted the earthquakes as "powerful spiritual signs," and "many Indians interpreted the quake as the [calamitous] anger of the great spirit at the loss of Indian culture and Indian lands" (Lecture, "New Madrid Earthquakes"). This interpretation fed three movements: the confederation led by the Shawnee brothers, including **Tecumseh**; a religious revival among the **Cherokee**; and a military strike among the **Creeks**. Through them, Native people were "[reaffirming] relationships with tradition and land strained by American territorial and cultural pressures" (Lecture, "New Madrid Earthquakes"). Among the Cherokee, the quakes deepened a "wave of [prophetic] visions and community revival" that had begun in 1811. Some Cherokees saw the events as apocalyptic, some as a sign "to resist Americanization" and "the pressures of dislocation," and others as "a symbol of anger from the great spirit [directed at] white settlers who are encroaching upon our land" (Lecture, "New Madrid Earthquakes").

White settlers and traders also gave the earthquakes spiritual meaning, largely through what they physically felt, heard, and smelled. The quakes released a sulfur stench over the region, and for people who lived through them, the shaking was "a kind of terrifying roar of underground thunder that represented the threat of damnation to fearful Christians on the frontier." Since "the devil smells of burning sulfur," many wondered whether this was God "shaking the land" with hellfire threatening to erupt from the earth (Lecture, "New Madrid Earthquakes"). These readings were strengthened by the **Second Great Awakening**, a religious movement of the early 1800s that stressed personal salvation and emotional revivals. At revivals across Tennessee and Kentucky, a sermon could move believers "to weeping to shouting to shaking to collapsing on the ground" (Lecture, "New Madrid Earthquakes"). The shaking earth was therefore "a direct parallel to the shaking and the way that... the Holy Spirit [registers] in your body," which helped people understand the earthquakes in religious terms (Lecture, "New Madrid Earthquakes"). The earthquakes held spiritual significance because both Native peoples and white settlers already viewed the world through spiritual frameworks: for Native communities these were shaped by the loss of land and culture, and for white settlers by frontier Christianity and revivalism.
